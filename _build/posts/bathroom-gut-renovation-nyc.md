## What a bathroom gut renovation includes

The point of a gut is the blank slate. Everything comes out, and the layout can change if you want it to. A typical scope covers:

- **Demolition and disposal.** Tile, tub or shower, vanity, toilet, flooring, wall surfaces and usually the ceiling, carried out within the building's allowed hours.
- **Plumbing.** New supply lines, drain and vent work where needed, a new shower valve, and relocated fixtures if the layout changes.
- **Electrical.** Updated circuits, GFCI protection, a properly ducted exhaust fan, and new lighting, including dimmable or controlled lighting.
- **Carpentry and framing.** Repairing or reframing walls and subfloor, and adding blocking for grab bars, glass and heavy vanities.
- **Waterproofing.** A membrane system in the shower and on the floor, tested before tile goes on.
- **Finishes.** Tile, glass, vanity, countertop, plumbing fixtures, mirrors, accessories and paint.

### How it differs from a partial remodel

A cosmetic refresh keeps the walls and the plumbing and changes surfaces and fixtures. A partial remodel replaces a few major items, such as the tub surround and the vanity. A gut is the only version that opens every wall and the floor.

That matters when the reason for the project is hidden: old galvanized or cast-iron piping, a slow leak, a failing shower pan, piecemeal wiring, or a layout that doesn't work. A partial remodel covers those problems up. A gut fixes them.

## When a gut is the right call

Three situations usually point to a full gut:

- **You're changing the layout.** Moving the shower, swapping a tub for a walk-in, or relocating the vanity means new drain lines and new framing.
- **Water has already done damage.** A soft floor around the toilet, a musty smell behind the tile, or stains on the ceiling of the apartment below all mean the room needs to be opened.
- **The systems are at the end of their life.** In prewar Manhattan co-ops, Brooklyn brownstones and older Queens buildings, the bathroom is where decades-old plumbing and wiring meet. Replacing them while the walls are open costs far less than coming back later.

## The right order of work

Most bathroom problems are sequencing problems: tile set before the inspection, an electrician called back to cut into a finished wall, a fan with no duct path. A gut should run roughly like this:

1. **Walkthrough and scope.** Measure, photograph, check access and building rules, and settle the layout before anything is ordered.
2. **Approvals.** Building paperwork, permits where required, and insurance certificates in place before work starts.
3. **Protection and demolition.** Hallways and the elevator are protected, the room is stripped to studs, and debris leaves within allowed hours.
4. **Framing and repairs.** Fix the subfloor, straighten walls, add blocking.
5. **Plumbing rough-in.** New supply, drain and vent lines and the shower valve body.
6. **Electrical rough-in.** Circuits, boxes, fan and lighting wiring, plus low-voltage runs for any controls or speakers.
7. **Inspections.** Open-wall work is checked while it's still visible.
8. **Board and waterproofing.** Wall board goes up, the membrane goes on, and the shower is flood-tested where the system calls for it.
9. **Tile and stone.** Floor and walls, then grout and sealing.
10. **Fixtures, glass, vanity and trim.** Plumbing and electrical finish work, mirrors, accessories and paint.
11. **Final walkthrough.** Punch list, then sign-off.

We go into more detail in [the right order of trades in a bathroom renovation](/blog/bathroom-renovation-order-of-trades/).

### Hidden problems to expect

Opening the walls is when surprises show up, especially in older buildings:

- Corroded or undersized supply and drain piping.
- Subfloor that has been wet for years around the toilet or tub.
- Old waterproofing that was never installed correctly behind the old tile.
- Wiring added piecemeal over the decades.
- Walls out of plumb, which affects large tile and glass.

A good contract says in writing how these discoveries will be handled before demolition, not in the middle of it.

## NYC permits, licensed trades and inspections

New York City treats plumbing and electrical work as licensed trade work. Replacing a faucet or toilet like for like is very different from relocating fixtures, running new drains or adding circuits. The second kind generally involves licensed plumbers and electricians, filings with the Department of Buildings, and inspections. What applies depends on the scope and the building, so confirm it before work starts.

## Gutting a bathroom in a co-op or condo

In a co-op or condo, the building has a say before the city does. Expect:

- **Alteration agreement.** A formal application describing the scope, with drawings for anything involving plumbing or layout.
- **Contractor documents.** The license and certificates of insurance naming the building and the managing agent.
- **Work hours and rules.** Weekday windows, no weekends or holidays, elevator padding, debris rules and noise limits.
- **Wet-area rules.** Some buildings won't let a bathroom move over a neighbor's living space, or require specific waterproofing and sound control.
- **Approval timing.** Boards meet on their own calendars. Submit early, because approval can take longer than design.

Fewer working hours per week means a bathroom in a Manhattan co-op usually takes longer on site than the same room in a Staten Island house with open access. ## How long a bathroom gut takes

There isn't one honest number. Duration depends on layout changes, material lead times, inspections and building hours. Here's where the time goes:

- **Before demolition:** design decisions, ordering tile, vanity, glass and fixtures, and building approvals. Special-order items have the longest lead times, so order early.
- **Demolition and rough-in:** usually the fastest and noisiest stretch.
- **Inspection and waterproofing:** can't be skipped or rushed, and cure times matter.
- **Tile and finishes:** the longest visible stretch, especially with intricate patterns or custom glass.

We give a schedule after the walkthrough. Settling every selection before demolition is the best way to avoid delays.

## What you can do yourself

Choosing finishes, clearing the room, painting and hanging accessories are all fine for a homeowner. The work inside the walls is different. Plumbing rough-in, wet-room electrical and waterproofing are where mistakes show up months later as leaks, mold or failed inspections, and in NYC they're the parts the licensing rules and your building are there to protect.

## Questions people ask

### Is a gut renovation the same as a complete bathroom remodel?

Usually, yes. Both mean taking the room down to the studs and replacing all materials and fixtures. Contractors tend to say "gut"; homeowners say "full" or "complete" remodel.

### Do I need a permit to gut a bathroom in NYC?

It depends on scope. Cosmetic swaps often don't need one, but moving fixtures, changing drains or adding circuits generally involves licensed trades and Department of Buildings filings. We confirm what applies at the walkthrough and handle the filings.

### Can I renovate a bathroom in a co-op without board approval?

Plan on needing approval. Most co-ops and condos require an alteration agreement, proof of license and insurance, and compliance with work-hour rules before demolition starts. Check your proprietary lease or house rules and submit early.

### How much should I budget for a bathroom gut renovation in NYC?

For a gut with basic finishes, plan on at least $15,000 to $25,000. Luxury bathrooms with stone, custom glass, steam or layout changes have no real ceiling. A walkthrough is the only way to get a firm number for your room.

### Can I do part of the work myself?

Yes, for finishes like paint and accessories. Plumbing, electrical and waterproofing should be done by licensed professionals, especially in New York, where the work is regulated and your building will want documentation.
