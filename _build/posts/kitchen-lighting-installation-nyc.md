## Start with layers, not fixtures

The most common kitchen mistake is one ceiling fixture doing every job. [HGTV's kitchen lighting guide](https://www.hgtv.com/decorating/kitchens/how-to-choose-kitchen-lighting) breaks a kitchen into four layers:

- **Ambient:** the base layer. Recessed lights, a flush ceiling fixture or indirect pendants.
- **Task:** over the sink, the range and every stretch of counter where you prep and plate. Under-cabinet strips and well-placed recessed lights do most of this work.
- **Accent:** glass-front cabinets, toe-kicks, open shelves, a feature wall or the inside of a pantry.
- **Decorative:** pendants over an island or a sconce by a window. They add character and some light, but shouldn't carry the room alone.

[Fine Homebuilding](https://www.finehomebuilding.com/project-guides/kitchens/kitchen-lighting-design) sets the floor at ambient plus task lighting, and notes that in a simple plan dimmable recessed fixtures can provide both. A recessed light aimed at art or an architectural detail can even count as accent light. If the budget is tight, start there and leave spare capacity for the layers you add later.

### How bright should it be?

Illuminating Engineering Society guidance, as summarized in a [Penn State energy course](https://courses.ems.psu.edu/egee102/node/2363), calls for roughly 30 to 40 foot-candles of general kitchen light and 50 to 100 foot-candles at task areas like the stove and sink. In plain terms, work surfaces should be noticeably brighter than the rest of the room. That's why dimmers matter: a kitchen bright enough to break down a chicken is too bright for a glass of water at midnight.

## Where each light goes

Placement matters more than fixture count. As This Old House general contractor Tom Silva puts it in their [recessed lighting guide](https://www.thisoldhouse.com/lighting/how-to-install-recessed-lighting-in-a-kitchen), "With kitchen lighting, positioning is everything."

### Recessed and ceiling lights

Stand at the counter with a light directly overhead and your head and shoulders block it, leaving your hands in shadow. The fix is to bring the light forward of where you stand, so the beam lands on the counter in front of you rather than on your back.

For spacing, This Old House gives roughly 4 to 6 feet between recessed lights in a grid as a common rule of thumb. Treat that as a starting point. In a galley or L-shaped kitchen, it usually means one row along each counter run plus a few lights in the center, not a neat checkerboard.

NYC apartments add their own constraints. Ceilings are often low, and soffits, beams and old pipe runs get in the way. In many high-rises the ceiling is a concrete slab with no cavity at all. A recessed light needs depth above the ceiling plane, so slim LED fixtures, surface-mounted discs or a dropped soffit are common answers. We check what's above the ceiling before anyone commits to a layout.

### Under-cabinet lighting

This is the most useful task light in most kitchens, because it sits between you and the work. This Old House's [under-cabinet LED guide](https://www.thisoldhouse.com/kitchens/how-to-install-undercabinet-led-lighting) recommends mounting strips 1 to 2 inches back from the face frame so the light spreads across the backsplash and countertop, and choosing strips of at least 200 lumens per foot.

Two details from the field:

- Measure each cabinet run separately. Breaks, corners and changes in depth all affect the strip lengths.
- Decide where the power comes from before the backsplash goes up. A hardwired driver tucked into a cabinet on a switched or dimmed feed looks far cleaner than a plug-in kit with a cord running down the tile.

### Pendants over an island or peninsula

Lower pendants make a stronger statement but block sight lines across the room; higher ones disappear. The right height depends on the fixture, the ceiling and who's tallest in the house. Hang a test height with string or a temporary cord before the electrician sets the box.

## Fixtures, color and controls

### Color temperature and CRI

Most homes use warm-white light, and some designers go slightly cooler over work surfaces for contrast. The rule that matters most: keep every fixture in the room at a matching color temperature, or plan any difference on purpose. A warm pendant next to a cool under-cabinet strip looks like a mistake. Check the color rendering index (CRI) too. A low-CRI strip makes food look dull and gray.

### Dimmers and scenes

Put each layer on its own control. A basic setup:

- Recessed ambient lights on one dimmer.
- Under-cabinet task lights on a second.
- Pendants on a third.
- Accent lighting on a fourth, or tied to the under-cabinet circuit.

Past three or four circuits, individual switches turn into a wall of plates. That's when a lighting control system earns its place: one keypad with scenes such as Cooking, Dining and Cleanup. Our guide to [lighting control installation in NYC](/blog/lighting-control-installation-nyc/) covers how that works.

Check compatibility early. Not every LED fixture dims smoothly on every dimmer, so the driver, dimmer and fixture should be specified as a set.

## The installation sequence in a remodel

Lighting goes smoothly when it follows the order of the remodel itself. This is the sequence we plan around:

1. **Layout and fixture schedule.** Every fixture is marked on the plan, with heights and dimmer groups, before demolition ends.
2. **Rough-in.** With walls and ceilings open, the electrician runs cable, sets boxes and adds blocking for pendants or heavy fixtures. This is also the moment to pre-wire for future controls. See [what to pre-wire before the drywall goes up](/blog/what-to-prewire-before-drywall/).
3. **Inspection where required.** Depending on the category of work, the city may need to inspect before the walls close (more below).
4. **Close-up and finishes.** Drywall, cabinets, countertops and backsplash. Under-cabinet strips go in after the cabinets are set and before the backsplash, or are routed into a channel.
5. **Trim-out.** Fixtures, drivers, dimmers and pendants are installed and programmed, then aimed and adjusted.

## What needs a licensed electrician in NYC

In New York City the line is clear. According to the [NYC Department of Buildings](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/property-or-business-owner/project-requirements-owner-electrical.page), no installation or modification of an electrical system can be done without a permit and a Licensed Master Electrician. That covers new circuits, new switch legs, new recessed lights and hardwired under-cabinet systems. Even minor work, such as listed replacements and repairs, needs the electrician and a permit, just not a DOB inspection.

The DOB's [electrical work guidance](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/buildings/pdf/elect_work_guidance.pdf) sets out three categories under the 2025 NYC Electrical Code:

| Category | Permit | DOB inspection | Plan review |
|---|---|---|---|
| Minor Electrical Work | Yes | No | No |
| Electrical Work | Yes | Yes | No |
| Electrical Work including Special Installations | Yes | Yes | Yes |

A kitchen remodel that adds circuits or moves lighting generally lands in the middle category, but the electrician determines the category for your scope and files accordingly.

The building has its own layer of rules. In a Manhattan co-op or a Queens condo, the board will usually need to approve the work under your alteration agreement, and it will typically require certificates of insurance, limit work to weekday hours and require freight elevator reservations. Some alteration agreements cover even modest changes, so ask management before you buy plug-in or battery under-cabinet kits. In a Brooklyn brownstone or a Staten Island house you skip the board, but old wiring and plaster ceilings often turn up once the walls open.

What's left for you: choosing fixtures, finishes, color temperature and the layout you want. Anything that involves opening a wall, touching the panel or extending a circuit is licensed work.

One code point to design around: the National Electrical Code requires at least one wall-switch-controlled lighting outlet in every habitable room, kitchen and bathroom of a dwelling. In practice, a smart bulb or an app can't be a kitchen's only control. Plan a proper wall control at the entrance.

## What affects cost and timing

We don't publish a flat price for kitchen lighting because it depends on scope. These are the factors that move the number:

- **Number of circuits and controls.** Four dimmed zones cost more than one switched ceiling light, and a scene keypad system adds equipment and programming.
- **Ceiling and wall conditions.** Plaster, masonry, concrete slab ceilings, soffits and old wiring all take more labor than open drywall framing.
- **Fixture selection.** Decorative pendants, custom channels and tunable-white strips vary widely.
- **Panel capacity.** An older apartment panel may need an upgrade to carry new circuits. See our guide to [electrical panel upgrades in NYC](/blog/electrical-panel-upgrade-nyc/).
- **Building rules.** Limited work hours, elevator reservations and alteration-agreement approvals all affect the schedule.
- **Permits and inspections.** Filing and inspection take calendar time, so the lighting rough-in belongs on the schedule from day one.

The most reliable way to control cost is to settle the lighting plan before demolition. Changes after the cabinets are in mean opening finished surfaces. When lighting is part of the kitchen design from the first walkthrough, it's a line item instead of a change order. ## FAQ

### How far apart should kitchen recessed lights be?

A common starting point is about 4 to 6 feet apart, according to This Old House. Place them forward of where you stand at the counter so your body doesn't shadow the work, then adjust for the real layout: islands, cabinets and whatever is above the ceiling.

### What color temperature is best for kitchen lighting?

Most homes use warm-white light, and some designers go slightly cooler over work surfaces. Whatever you choose, keep the fixtures in one space consistent and check that the CRI is high enough to show food and finishes accurately.

### Do I need a permit to install kitchen lighting in NYC?

Yes. Per the DOB, electrical installation or modification in NYC requires a permit and a Licensed Master Electrician. The category of work decides whether a DOB inspection or plan review is also needed. In a co-op or condo, check your alteration agreement as well.

### When should kitchen lighting be planned in a remodel?

At the design stage, before demolition ends and well before drywall and cabinets. Fixture locations, dimmer groups and wall controls drive the rough-in wiring. Changing them later means opening finished walls and ceilings.
