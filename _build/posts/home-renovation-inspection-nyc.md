## The three inspections around a renovation

| Stage | Who does it | What it answers | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-renovation | A licensed home inspector, an engineer or architect, or your contractor's walkthrough | What shape are the plumbing, wiring, framing and finishes in? | Before design and pricing are final |
| Permit inspections | DOB inspectors, or a registered design professional for some work types | Was regulated work installed to code before it was covered? | During construction, at set stages |
| Closeout | You and your project lead, plus an independent inspector if you want one | Is the work finished, working and documented? | At completion |

People mix these up. A DOB sign-off confirms that permitted work meets code. It doesn't tell you whether the grout lines are straight or the fan actually vents outside. A private inspector looks at condition, not code approval. You want both kinds of eyes on a large project.

## Why inspect before you renovate

The cheapest time to find a problem is before anyone has priced the job. In New York City's older housing stock, the usual surprises are behind the plaster:

- **Budget accuracy.** Rotted subfloor under a prewar bathroom, galvanized supply lines in a Brooklyn brownstone, or an undersized panel in a Queens two-family are much cheaper to plan for than to find after demolition.
- **Sequencing.** If the panel, risers or drain lines need work, that work goes into the schedule before finishes, not after.
- **A baseline.** You know what condition the home was in on a given day, which helps when you compare it with the finished work.

### What to look at for a kitchen or bathroom

For a kitchen or bath, the pre-renovation look should cover water supply and drains, the electrical panel and circuits, exhaust ventilation, subfloor and framing, signs of past leaks or active moisture, and, in a co-op or condo, which pipes are yours and which belong to the building.

### Who can do it in New York

For one- to four-family homes, New York State requires anyone doing [home inspections for pay to be licensed by the Department of State](https://dos.ny.gov/frequently-asked-questions-home-inspector). Licensed architects and engineers working within their profession are exempt. If you own a brownstone or a house on [Staten Island](/areas/staten-island/) or in [Queens](/areas/queens/), ask for the inspector's license number.

A home inspection is a visual review of what can be seen and reached. It won't open walls, and it isn't a code approval. When a report says "further evaluation recommended" for the wiring or drains, the next step is a licensed electrician or plumber who can open things up, price the fix and file the permit.

## Red flags in previously renovated homes

If you're buying a renovated apartment or house, or looking at someone else's earlier work, be skeptical of fresh finishes. The American Society of Home Inspectors warns that house flippers [often make cosmetic changes while ignoring or concealing serious issues](https://www.homeinspector.org/reporter-articles/flipped-home-inspections). A new kitchen tells you nothing about the plumbing behind it.

Things we look for when we open up earlier work:

- Double-tapped breakers or an overfilled panel
- Three-prong outlets with no ground and no GFCI protection
- Mixed pipe materials joined without proper fittings
- Bathroom fans that vent into the ceiling instead of outside
- Stains or soft spots below wet rooms
- Work that obviously needed a permit with no permit on record

## DOB inspections: what happens while the walls are open

In NYC, the rule of thumb is simple: permitted work gets inspected before it's covered, and the walls don't close until it passes.

**Plumbing.** The DOB says the Licensed Master Plumber [must obtain permits and arrange for tests and inspections of completed work by a DOB inspector](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/property-or-business-owner/plumbing-permits.page). The [plumbing inspection requirements](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/industry/project-requirements-registrant-plumbing-n-insp.page) call for inspections at several stages, including roughing piping in floors, ceilings and walls, and a final sign-off. For a bathroom, the tub or shower body and pressure-balancing valve piping have to be in place for the rough inspection.

**Electrical.** Electrical work needs a permit and a Licensed Master Electrician. The DOB says electricians [cannot self-certify the completion of electrical work](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/industry/project-requirements-skilled-trades-electrical-n-insp.page), so a DOB final inspection, scheduled in DOB NOW, is required. See our [electrical panel upgrade guide](/blog/electrical-panel-upgrade-nyc/) for how that plays out on a service upgrade.

**Construction.** For general construction alterations, the [DOB NOW: Build FAQ](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/industry/dob-now-build-faqs.page) says the final inspection may be done by the DOB or by a registered design professional, chosen on the filing. Structural, foundation and mechanical work types are finished by a registered design professional. Your architect or contractor should tell you which applies before the job starts.

A typical gut renovation runs in this order:

1. Board approval and DOB permits in place, then demolition.
2. Rough plumbing, electrical, HVAC and low-voltage installed.
3. Rough inspections passed. Nothing gets covered before this.
4. Insulation, then walls and ceilings closed.
5. Tile, cabinets, fixtures and devices installed.
6. Final plumbing and electrical inspections, then the construction sign-off.

If you want an extra layer, a private pre-drywall inspection fits at step 3. InterNACHI describes it as happening [before the insulation and interior walls and ceilings are covered](https://www.nachi.org/understanding-types-code-inspections.htm), when problems are still visible and cheap to fix.

### What NYC buildings add

In co-ops and condos, the board or managing agent usually has to approve an alteration agreement before work starts. Buildings set their own work hours, insurance certificate requirements and freight elevator bookings. The DOB inspection date and the building's work hours both constrain the schedule. A rough-in that has to stay visible for an inspector can't be closed early, so the order of trades matters. ## The closeout walkthrough

After a large project, a final walkthrough is worth an hour of your time. An independent re-inspection is optional; it's a second set of eyes, not a warranty.

Use this list:

- Confirm every required DOB inspection was passed and signed off, and keep copies.
- In a co-op or condo, get whatever closeout paperwork the building requires.
- Run every faucet, shower and drain, and check under sinks and at the shutoffs.
- Test every GFCI outlet and switch, and confirm the panel directory is labeled.
- Check that exhaust fans move air and vent outside.
- Watch the ceilings below the bathroom and kitchen for stains over the next few weeks.
- Write a punch list and agree on a date for each item.

## What to ask your contractor

A contractor's walkthrough is a normal part of scoping and pricing a job. It isn't the same as an independent inspection report or a DOB sign-off. Ask:

- Who files the permits, and who schedules each inspection?
- Which trades are licensed, and who's on site for rough inspections?
- If an inspection fails, who fixes it and who pays for the re-inspection?
- What do you need from me for the alteration agreement and insurance certificates?
- What goes behind the walls that I won't be able to reach later?

That last question matters for technology. Wiring for lighting control, shades, audio and networking is easiest to run while walls are open. See [what to pre-wire before the drywall goes up](/blog/what-to-prewire-before-drywall/).

## FAQ

### Do I need a home inspection before a renovation?

For a major project, yes. It shows the condition of the plumbing, wiring, framing and moisture before design and pricing are locked in. It's most useful in older NYC buildings, where the systems are hidden behind plaster.

### What inspections does the NYC DOB require during a renovation?

It depends on the permit. Plumbing work is inspected at the rough stage and at final sign-off, arranged by the Licensed Master Plumber. Electrical work needs a DOB final inspection, because electricians can't self-certify completion. General construction alterations close out with a final inspection by the DOB or a registered design professional.

### Can walls be closed before the inspection?

No. Rough plumbing and other permitted work that will be concealed needs to stay visible until it's inspected and passed. Closing it early can mean opening finished walls back up.

### Does a home inspector need a license in New York?

For one- to four-family homes, yes. New York State requires paid home inspectors to be licensed by the Department of State. Licensed architects and engineers working within their profession are exempt.

### Do I need an inspection after the renovation is finished?

The required ones are the DOB sign-offs, so keep copies. An independent walkthrough after a big project is optional but useful for checking workmanship. It doesn't guarantee the work.
