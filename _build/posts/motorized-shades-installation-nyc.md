## First decision: how the shades get power

Before you measure anything, decide how each shade will be powered. Most motorized systems offer some version of three options, and each leads to a different installation.

**Battery wand or built-in battery.** Nothing is wired. The shade mounts like a manual one, and you charge or replace batteries on a schedule. It's the simplest install and the easiest to retrofit into an occupied apartment. The tradeoff is upkeep: with a dozen windows, the battery routine becomes a chore, and a tall window makes every swap a ladder job.

**Remote battery pack.** The pack sits away from the headrail, which keeps the shade clean-looking and puts the battery within reach. It still avoids house wiring, but it adds a small cable run along the window or wall that has to be routed neatly.

**Low-voltage hardwired supply.** A power supply feeds the motors through low-voltage wiring. No batteries, ever. The catch: wire has to reach every window, and the supply itself connects to household power. That connection is electrical work.

If you're renovating anyway, hardwiring is the better long-term answer for living rooms, bedrooms and large windows. Pulling wire with the wall open takes a fraction of the effort of fishing it through finished plaster in a prewar building. We cover this in [what to pre-wire before the drywall goes up](/blog/what-to-prewire-before-drywall/).

## How motorized shades are installed, step by step

This sequence applies to most roller, cellular and similar motorized shades. Always follow the instructions for your specific product, because bracket spacing and motor setup differ between brands.

### 1. Measure and plan

Measure each opening in more than one place. Older NYC buildings are rarely square, and brownstone window openings can vary by more than you'd expect from top to bottom. Decide whether the shade goes inside the recess or on the wall or ceiling above it. Check what's behind the surface where the brackets go: wood framing, plaster over lath, a steel lintel, brick. That determines your fasteners.

Decide where the motor, the remote or wall control and any power source will sit before you drill. Moving a bracket later leaves holes in the trim.

### 2. Mark the brackets

Hold each bracket in place, level it and mark the holes. Brackets that aren't level are the most common reason a shade drifts to one side as it rolls. On a wide shade, a second person helps.

### 3. Drill and fasten

Reach wood framing where you can. A motorized shade is heavier than a manual one, and the motor adds torque every time it starts. In plaster or masonry, use the anchor made for that material, not a drywall anchor.

### 4. Mount the shade

Seat the headrail in the brackets and confirm it's centered over the opening. Don't pinch the cable between the headrail and the bracket. A pinched lead can fail months later.

### 5. Connect power

Battery shades: insert or connect the battery. Remote packs: plug in and route the cable. Hardwired shades: the electrician connects the power supply, and the shade plugs into its lead.

### 6. Set the limits and test

Run each shade fully up and down. If it stops short or hits the sill, adjust the upper and lower limits, usually in the manufacturer's app. Test from every control you plan to use (remote, wall keypad, app) before you put the ladder away. Troubleshooting is far easier while everything is still exposed.

### 7. Connect to the rest of the home

Most systems use an app for setup and a hub or gateway to talk to other systems. That's where scenes, schedules, voice control and lighting coordination come in, and it's where an install starts needing real programming.

## Tools and supplies

For a standard install: a drill and bits, a hex driver, a level, a tape measure, a pencil, screwdrivers, a stud finder, the right anchors for your wall and a stable ladder.

In a prewar apartment, add a masonry bit, masonry anchors and a vacuum. Plaster dust is unavoidable, and many buildings hold you responsible for cleaning hallways and elevators.

## Common installation mistakes

- **Brackets out of level.** The shade tracks to one side and the fabric rubs.
- **Weak anchoring.** Drywall anchors alone may not hold a heavy motorized shade. Reach framing where you can.
- **Pinched cables.** Snap the headrail in slowly and look before you press.
- **Skipping the limits.** A motor that runs too far wears the shade and the fabric.
- **Choosing the power source last.** Deciding on batteries or wire after the shades arrive limits your options and usually costs more.
- **Forgetting the controls.** A remote that lives in a drawer means a shade nobody uses. Plan wall keypads and scenes at the start.

## Can you install motorized shades yourself?

Often, yes, if they're battery powered. A careful homeowner with basic tools can handle a few battery shades in one room or a rental.

The line that matters is electrical. In NYC, the Department of Buildings requires [a Licensed Master Electrician and a permit](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/property-or-business-owner/project-requirements-owner-electrical.page) for installing or modifying any electrical system, including minor work. A new outlet or power supply for hardwired shades falls on that side of the line.

Bring in a professional when:

- The shades are hardwired and there's no power near the window.
- You want shades tied into lighting scenes, keypads or a whole-home system.
- The windows are tall, over a stair or tub, or otherwise hard to reach safely.
- There are skylights or oversize openings.
- You're renovating and want power, conduit and shade pockets built into the walls and ceiling.
- You live in a co-op or condo that requires licensed, insured trades.

## NYC specifics: permits, licenses and building rules

### Do motorized shades need a permit?

A straight swap of window coverings generally isn't treated as a home improvement job. The city requires a [Home Improvement Contractor license](https://portal.311.nyc.gov/article?kanumber=KA-01808) from the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection for residential work over $200, but Venetian blinds are on its list of work that doesn't need one. The wiring is a separate question: new circuits, power supplies or conduit are electrical work under a DOB electrical permit.

When shades are part of a bigger renovation, the permit question moves to the renovation. For alteration permits in one- to four-family homes and in co-op, condo and rented apartment units, [DOB requires contractors to provide their HIC license details](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/property-or-business-owner/home-improvement-contractor-permit-requirements.page). Some smaller alterations that don't change use, egress or occupancy can be filed under DOB's [Directive 14 limited-review program](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/buildings/pdf/code_notes_directive-14of1975-alterations.pdf), which still requires authorization from the co-op or condo board where applicable. Whether your scope qualifies depends on the exact work.

### Co-op and condo rules

In a Manhattan co-op or a Brooklyn condo, the building often has more say than the city. Expect an alteration agreement for anything beyond cosmetic work, a certificate of insurance naming the building and managing agent, proof of licensing and limits on work hours and elevator use. Some buildings also have rules about what window treatments look like from the street, such as the color of the shade backing. Ask your managing agent before you order.

If you're in the middle of board approval, our guide to the [co-op and condo alteration process](/blog/nyc-co-op-condo-renovation-alteration-process/) walks through what boards ask for and in what order.

### Work hours and logistics

Buildings commonly limit noisy work to weekday daytime hours and require freight elevator reservations and floor protection. Hanging a shade is quiet. Drilling into brick or fishing wire through plaster isn't. Schedule so the electrician, the shade installer and the painter aren't on top of each other.

## What affects the cost

We don't publish a price for motorized shades because the number depends almost entirely on scope. These are the things that move it:

- **Number and size of windows.** More windows and wider shades mean more motors, fabric and labor.
- **Power type.** Battery shades cost less to install. Hardwired systems add wiring, a power supply and electrician time, but no battery upkeep.
- **Fabric and style.** Blackout, solar, sheer, cellular and dual shades price differently, and custom sizes add to the total.
- **Controls and integration.** A basic remote is simple. Scenes, keypads, voice control and whole-home integration add programming.
- **Access and building conditions.** Tall windows, skylights, plaster or masonry walls and building restrictions all add labor.
- **Timing.** Shades powered during a renovation cost far less to wire than shades retrofitted into finished walls.

National cost guides give wide ranges because these variables are so large, and NYC labor and building requirements run higher than national averages. Treat any national figure as a rough starting point, not a quote for a prewar Upper West Side apartment or a Park Slope brownstone. Ask for a written scope that separates shades, power, controls and programming, so you can compare bids line by line.

## Drawbacks to weigh

- **Batteries.** Battery models need charging or replacement, and tall windows make that awkward.
- **Wiring.** Hardwired shades need an electrician and a way to run wire, which is hardest in finished walls.
- **Programming.** The more advanced the automation, the more setup it takes.
- **Safety.** A moving shade can pinch fingers or catch a pet or an object, and scheduled shades move when no one is watching. Keep the travel path clear, and use obstacle detection where the product offers it, especially in rooms with kids or pets.
- **Repairs.** Motors and electronics fail eventually. Choose products with a clear support and replacement path.

## FAQ

### Can I install motorized blinds myself?

Yes, if they're battery powered: mark and drill the brackets, mount the headrail, power the shade and set the limits. If they're hardwired, the electrical connection in NYC needs a Licensed Master Electrician and a permit.

### How long does motorized shade installation take?

It depends on the product and power type. Battery shades usually go up quickly, window by window. Hardwired and integrated systems take longer because of wiring, inspection, programming and testing. A realistic schedule comes after someone sees the windows and the building's rules.

### Do I need an electrician for motorized shades?

Not for battery shades. Yes for hardwired motors or any new power supply, because those connect to household power. In NYC that means a Licensed Master Electrician under a DOB electrical permit.

### Do I need my co-op or condo board's permission?

Very often, if you're bringing in contractors or doing any wiring. Boards commonly require an alteration agreement, an insurance certificate and licensed trades, and they limit work hours. Ask your managing agent before you order anything.

### What are the downsides of motorized shades?

Batteries need upkeep, hardwiring needs an electrician, and advanced automation takes more programming. Moving shades also need a clear path, since scheduled shades can start when no one is in the room.
