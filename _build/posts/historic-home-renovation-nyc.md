## Start by understanding what you own

The most expensive mistakes on old buildings happen in the first week, when someone starts clearing out and tearing off. The National Park Service's [Preservation Brief 35 on architectural investigation](https://www.nps.gov/orgs/1739/upload/preservation-brief-35-architectural-investigation.pdf) lays out four steps: historical research, documentation, inventory and stabilization. It warns that premature clean-up can be a mistake, and that anything that seems meaningful should be saved, labeled and, where possible, photographed before it goes into storage.

For a Brooklyn brownstone, a Harlem rowhouse, a prewar apartment on the Upper West Side or an older house on Staten Island, that means:

- **Research the history.** The same brief notes that deed records establish the sequence of owners and help identify what each occupant changed. That gives you a framework before physical work begins.
- **Photograph and measure everything.** Moldings, door hardware, original floors, plaster profiles, window sash and radiators all belong in the record.
- **Get a professional look at existing conditions.** The National Trust for Historic Preservation says a professional [should be able to judge which structure, finishes and systems need to be restored, repaired or replaced](https://savingplaces.org/stories/10-on-tuesday-how-to-plan-your-restoration-or-rehabilitation-project). Its example is a good one: a masonry crack that looks alarming may be only cosmetic, while peeling paint can point to moisture building up inside the wall.
- **Stabilize first.** Roof, masonry and water coming in come before kitchens and finishes.

If you're an owner or investor, this is also when you find out whether the scope you budgeted is the scope the building needs. For what that walkthrough covers, see our guide to [home renovation inspections in NYC](/blog/home-renovation-inspection-nyc/).

## Find out whether the building is protected

The first New York question is whether your property is an individual landmark or sits in a designated historic district. If it does, you need an LPC permit before doing any work, and that includes any work that needs a DOB permit, according to the city's guidance on [performing work on a landmarked property](https://nyc-business.nyc.gov/nycbusiness/description/performing-work-on-a-landmarked-property). LPC issues three kinds of permit:

| Permit | When it applies | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| Certificate of No Effect (CNE) | Work needs a DOB permit but doesn't change protected architectural features | Interior renovations, installing plumbing and heating equipment |
| Permit for Minor Work (PMW) | Work changes significant protected features but doesn't need a DOB permit | Replacing windows or doors, cleaning or repairing masonry, restoring details |
| Certificate of Appropriateness (CofA) | Work changes significant protected features and doesn't meet the guidelines for staff-level approval | Rooftop or rear-yard additions, demolition, removing stoops or cornices |

A Certificate of Appropriateness goes to a public hearing before the full Commission. LPC charges a filing fee based on the cost of work for most permits, and none for a Permit for Minor Work; check the current amounts on [LPC's fee page](https://www.nyc.gov/site/lpc/applications/fees.page) before you file.

Two practical points follow. Interior-only work in a landmark is often a CNE, but you still need that paperwork before the DOB filing moves. And exterior items people treat as routine, such as windows, front doors and masonry cleaning, are exactly where a Permit for Minor Work comes in. Don't order materials until approvals are in hand.

If you don't know whether your address is protected, check LPC's records before you sign anything, and have your architect or expediter confirm it in writing. Much of the protected housing stock is in [Brooklyn](/areas/brooklyn/) and [Manhattan](/areas/manhattan/), but there are historic districts in [Queens](/areas/queens/) and on [Staten Island](/areas/staten-island/) too.

## Codes and permits still apply to old buildings

Historic doesn't mean exempt. The National Park Service's page on [codes and regulatory requirements](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/taxincentives/codes-regulatory-requirements.htm) lists accessibility, fire and life safety, hazardous materials, seismic upgrades and energy performance as areas a rehabilitation still has to address, and recommends talking to code officials early in planning. It also points out that prescriptive code requirements can lead to excessive removal of historic material, and that careful planning can minimize that.

In NYC that translates into a few routines:

- **DOB permits and licensed trades.** Plumbing and electrical work is done by licensed professionals under permit. When [plumbing](/services/plumbing/) and [electrical](/services/electrical/) sit inside the same contract as general construction, inspections happen in the right order.
- **Co-op and condo alteration agreements.** If your prewar apartment is in a co-op or condo, the board's alteration process comes first, along with building insurance certificates, work-hour rules and freight elevator bookings. Our guide to [the co-op and condo alteration process](/blog/nyc-co-op-condo-renovation-alteration-process/) walks through it.
- **Phased work.** The National Trust notes that most building permits are good for 12 months from issue, and that you should tell the permit department if you plan to phase work over several years. Phased restorations on a brownstone are common, so plan for it.

## Set priorities: repair, then reproduce, then replace

The preservation approach is consistent: repair and stabilize original material first, patch with compatible materials, reproduce missing pieces to match, and replace only what can't be saved. Where you can, make new work reversible, so it can come out later without damaging what was there.

In an older NYC home that usually means:

- **Plaster walls.** Patch and skim rather than tearing out to drywall, unless the wall is beyond repair or you need access for new systems. Three-coat plaster is denser and quieter than anything you'll replace it with.
- **Mantels, woodwork, stained glass.** Overpainted trim and mantels are often original underneath. Strip and test a small area before deciding anything is a loss.
- **Windows.** Interior storm windows can improve comfort and noise without replacing original sash. In a historic district, window changes also need LPC review.
- **Masonry and chimneys.** Water is the enemy of brick and brownstone. Have the masonry and chimney looked at before the rest of the budget is committed, so you know what's cosmetic and what's structural.
- **Floor plans.** Partitions that can come out later protect the original layout for the next owner.

The more original fabric you keep, the more the finished house is worth as a historic house, and the fewer approvals you have to negotiate.

## Plan modern systems before the walls close

This is where historic renovations most often go wrong. Old buildings have thick plaster, masonry, plank subfloors and few chases. If plumbing, electrical and low-voltage work are scheduled separately, each trade cuts its own path, and the building takes the damage.

A coordinated plan looks like this:

- **One set of drawings for all trades.** Risers, panel location, drain lines, lighting control wiring, shade pockets, speaker runs and network drops are laid out together, so each uses the least invasive route.
- **Rough-in in sequence.** Plumbing, electrical and low-voltage rough-in, then inspections, then plaster repair and finishes.
- **Technology decided early.** [Lighting control](/services/lighting-control/), motorized shades, audio and networking are planned before the walls close, not added after. In a plaster house, that's the difference between a clean retrofit and patched holes through original walls.
- **Concealed, reversible installation.** Keep new wiring and equipment out of original trim and decorative surfaces.

## Cost and tax credits

Historic work costs more than standard remodeling because of investigation, specialty repair and the approvals timeline. We price old buildings from a walkthrough and a defined scope, not a per-square-foot figure, because the scope isn't knowable until someone has looked behind the plaster.

Tax credits are where owners get confused:

- **Federal 20% credit.** Per the National Park Service's [eligibility requirements](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/taxincentives/eligibility-requirements.htm), the building must be listed in the National Register or certified as contributing to a registered historic district, the project must pass the substantial rehabilitation test, and the building must be in an income-producing, depreciable use for at least five years. Owner-occupied homes don't qualify. If you own an income-producing building, talk to a tax professional before you design the project around it.
- **New York State homeowner credit.** The state's [Historic Homeownership Rehabilitation Tax Credit](https://parks.ny.gov/preservation/programs/tax-credit/homeowner) is 20% of qualified rehabilitation expenses for a home you own and live in (non-income-producing second homes also qualify). The house must be listed in the State or National Register, individually or as a contributing building in a district, and located in a qualifying census tract. Qualified expenses must total $5,000 or more, with at least 5% spent on exterior work, and the project must be approved before work begins.

The rule both share is timing: approval before work. Confirm eligibility with the State Historic Preservation Office and your accountant before you sign a construction contract.

Ask any firm for completed projects in buildings like yours, references, and a plain explanation of how they bill. Meet the person who will actually run the job, and sign a contract that spells out scope, schedule and fees before work starts.

## FAQ

### Do I need LPC approval to renovate the inside of my NYC home?

If the building is a landmark or in a historic district, you need an LPC permit before any work that needs a DOB permit, including interior renovations. Interior work that doesn't change protected features is usually covered by a Certificate of No Effect. Confirm the building's status before filing.

### Can I get a federal tax credit for renovating my own historic house?

Not if you live in it. The federal 20% rehabilitation credit is for buildings in income-producing, depreciable use, so owner-occupied homes don't qualify. New York State has a separate homeowner credit with its own rules, and the project must be approved before work begins.

### Should I restore or replace original windows and plaster?

Repair original material first, match what's missing, and replace only what can't be saved. Interior storm windows are one way to improve performance without removing original sash. In a historic district, window changes also need LPC review.

### How long is a building permit good for on a phased restoration?

The National Trust notes most building permits are good for 12 months from issue. If you plan to phase a restoration over several years, tell the permit department and plan the filings around the phases.

### Why use one contractor for all trades on a historic home?

Old walls and floors have few paths for new pipes and wires. When plumbing, electrical and low-voltage work are planned and scheduled together under one contract, each trade uses the least invasive route and inspections happen in order.
