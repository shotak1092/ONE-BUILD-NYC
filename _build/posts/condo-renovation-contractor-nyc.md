## What a condo renovation contractor does in NYC

In a house, a contractor mostly deals with you and the city. In a condo or co-op there is a third party: the building. The work happens inside shared walls, around shared risers and through shared elevators, under rules written by a board and a managing agent.

A capable contractor typically handles:

- **Scope and pricing** for kitchens, bathrooms, gut renovations, flooring, built-ins and layout changes.
- **The board package:** scope of work, schedule, insurance certificates and license copies.
- **Drawings:** coordinating with your architect or engineer on what the board and the city will review.
- **Trades:** general construction, plumbing, electrical and low-voltage systems such as lighting control, shades, audio and networking.
- **Building logistics:** hallway and elevator protection, freight elevator bookings, delivery windows and debris removal.
- **Inspections and closeout,** so the work is signed off and the building gets what it needs.

## Can you renovate a condo you own?

Yes, with approvals. Owning the unit doesn't mean you can open walls on your own schedule. CityRealty describes the [alteration agreement](https://www.cityrealty.com/nyc/market-insight/features/get-to-know/steps-take-before-renovating-nyc-apartment-bring-your-architect-these-diamonds-rough/57643) as essentially a contract between the apartment owner and the board, setting which renovations are allowed and when work can happen.

Boards look hardest at work that touches shared infrastructure. Gas, water and sewage lines in an apartment building sit in fixed locations, so moving a kitchen or bathroom affects the whole building. Updating a bathroom in place is usually far less of an issue. Every building writes its own rules, so read yours before you design anything.

A leak from one unit can damage the units below, which is why boards care about who does the plumbing. Expect to be asked whether the plumber is licensed and insured. ## Approvals in the right order

The sequence is where many owners trip. Board approval and city approval are two separate tracks, and since early 2026 they are tied together.

The Department of Buildings says [project approval is required before work begins](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/property-or-business-owner/project-checklists-owner-alt-multiple-dwelling.page), and that only a registered design professional, a Professional Engineer or Registered Architect, can submit construction documents.

Starting February 2, 2026, the [Condo/Co-op Board became a stakeholder](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/buildings/pdf/co-op_board-sn.pdf) on DOB NOW: Build filings made by a condo unit owner or co-op shareholder. A board representative has to attest that the board authorized the applicant to file. [Habitat magazine reports](https://www.habitatmag.com/Archive2/441-March-2026/DOB-Authorization) that if a board flags an unauthorized filing, DOB holds the application, or issues a Stop Work Order if the permit is already out. In practice, that means you settle things with the board first.

A sequence that works:

1. **Read the alteration agreement and house rules.** Note work hours, elevator reservations, deposits and insurance wording.
2. **Hire a design professional if your scope needs a filing.** Cosmetic work may not. Moving walls, plumbing or gas usually does.
3. **Get contractor pricing against drawings.** A price without a defined scope is a guess.
4. **Submit the board package.** Expect questions and resubmissions.
5. **File with DOB, get the board's attestation and receive the permit.** Permitted work doesn't start before this.
6. **Build, inspect, close out.** Inspections are scheduled around trade progress, not after it.

Board calendars and DOB review times are the two biggest unknowns. Ask your contractor and architect for a realistic range for your building, not a single promised date.

## Licenses and insurance to verify before you sign

### The home improvement license

The city says a contractor needs a [Home Improvement Contractor license](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dca/downloads/pdf/consumers/Home-Improvement-Contractor-Tips.pdf) for work over $200 on any residential building in NYC. That license doesn't cover new home construction, plumbing or purely electrical work. One Build NYC holds NYC Home Improvement Contractor License #621290. Whoever you hire, get the number and [check it with the city](https://www.nyc.gov/site/dca/consumers/shopping-services-home-improvement.page) yourself, online or by calling 311.

It matters beyond paperwork. The city warns that if you use an unlicensed contractor you aren't eligible for repayment through its Home Improvement Contractor Trust Fund.

Plumbing and electrical are separately licensed trades, so the licensed professionals doing that work should be named in your paperwork too. Having all of them under one contract gives the board one point of accountability.

### Building insurance certificates

CityRealty notes that most condos and co-ops require contractors to carry at least $1,000,000 in insurance. The building, the managing agent and sometimes the board usually have to be named as additional insureds. Requirements differ, so get the exact wording from your managing agent before anyone orders a certificate. Wrong wording is a common reason a package gets kicked back.

### Payment and contract basics

The city's guidance is plain: never pay in cash or without a contract, and pay no more than 25% of the total upfront. The contract must be written and signed by both sides, and must describe the work, materials, payment schedule and all charges. Tie later payments to completed milestones, and put the change-order process in writing.

## How to compare contractors

| What to check | Why it matters in a condo | Good sign |
| --- | --- | --- |
| License and insurance | The board and the city both ask | Numbers and certificates come without delay |
| Building experience | Work hours, elevators and house rules shape the schedule | Asks for your alteration agreement in the first conversation |
| Trade structure | Handoffs between companies create gaps | One contract, one project lead, one schedule |
| Written scope | Prevents disputes over allowances and extras | Itemized scope with clear exclusions |
| Permit handling | Unpermitted work can stop a job and cause trouble at resale | Works with your design professional from the start |
| Communication | Boards and neighbors will call someone | A named point of contact |

Give every contractor the same drawings and the same finish list. Otherwise you're comparing different projects, not different prices.

## What moves the price of a condo renovation

There's no flat rate in NYC. We quote against a defined scope after a walkthrough, because every building and unit is different. These factors move the number most:

- **Scope.** A cosmetic refresh, a kitchen and bath remodel and a [gut renovation](/blog/cost-of-gut-renovation-nyc/) are three different projects.
- **Moving plumbing and gas.** Relocating a kitchen or bathroom adds trade work, filing and board review.
- **Building constraints.** Restricted hours, elevator reservations and long carries add labor.
- **Electrical capacity.** Panel upgrades and new circuits for appliances or smart systems.
- **Finishes.** Tile, stone, cabinetry, fixtures and appliances vary widely, and their lead times affect the schedule.
- **Design and filing fees.** Architects, engineers, expediters and board fees sit outside construction.
- **Contingency.** Older buildings hide surprises behind the walls. Keep a reserve.

## Where condo projects lose time

Most delays are about sequence, not craftsmanship.

- **Designing before reading the house rules.** A layout the board rejects costs weeks.
- **Incomplete board packages.** In a building whose board meets monthly, a small correction can turn into a long wait.
- **Trades booked separately.** When the plumber, electrician and installer answer to different companies, nobody owns the order of rough-in, inspection and close-up.
- **Late decisions.** Special-order tile, fixtures and appliances show up after the schedule assumed them.
- **Technology added after the walls close.** Lighting control, motorized shades, speakers and network cable are simple to run before drywall and hard to retrofit.
- **Ignoring work hours.** The city [allows residential construction on weekdays, 7 AM to 6 PM](https://portal.311.nyc.gov/article/?kanumber=KA-01091), and many buildings set narrower windows. A task that takes a day elsewhere can take two.

## Questions to ask in the first meeting

- Have you worked in my building or similar ones, and what are its work-hour limits?
- Who is my single point of contact from start to finish?
- Which licensed trades do you manage directly, and which do you subcontract?
- Can you match my building's certificate requirements exactly?
- How do you handle change orders and hidden conditions?
- What's the payment schedule, and does it keep the upfront payment to 25% or less?
- When do lighting, shades, audio and network cable go into the schedule?

A contractor who answers these clearly and in writing has usually dealt with a real building's rules before.

## FAQ

### Do I need a licensed contractor to renovate my condo?

Home improvement work over $200 on a residential building in NYC requires a Home Improvement Contractor license, while plumbing and electrical work are licensed separately. Your building will almost certainly ask for licenses and insurance before it approves anything.

### What comes first, the board or the city permit?

Start with the board and the alteration agreement, then file with DOB, usually through a registered architect or engineer. Since February 2026 a board representative also has to attest in DOB NOW that the board authorized the filing. Don't start permitted work until both are in place.

### Is a condo renovation contractor the same as an apartment renovation company?

Functionally, yes. Both do interior work inside a multi-unit building. What matters is the building: condos and co-ops have boards, alteration agreements and insurance rules, so choose a firm that works within them regularly.

### How much can I pay upfront?

NYC's consumer guidance says no more than 25% of the total contract amount, never in cash and always under a written contract. Tie remaining payments to milestones.
