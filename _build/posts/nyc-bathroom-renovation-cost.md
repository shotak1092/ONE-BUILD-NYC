## What a bathroom renovation costs in NYC

National cost guides don't describe New York prices. Licensed plumbers and electricians cost more here, materials go up freight elevators and walk-up stairs, co-op and condo buildings limit work hours, and boards require insurance certificates, protection of common areas and sometimes their own engineer's review. All of that is real cost, and none of it shows up in a national average.

Here's how our bathroom work prices out:

| Scope | Realistic budget |
|---|---|
| Gut remodel with basic finishes | At least $15,000 to $25,000 |
| Luxury bathroom (stone, custom glass, steam, heated floors, layout changes) | No real ceiling: finishes and scope decide it |

That's the starting range for a real gut, not a quote. We give a firm number after a walkthrough, once we've seen the room, the building and the access.

Why does a small room cost this much? A bathroom packs plumbing, waterproofing, tile, ventilation, electrical and fixtures into a few dozen square feet, and every one of those trades has to visit it in the right order. Per square foot, it's the most expensive room in the home.

## Refresh, partial remodel or full gut

The scope you choose sets the budget more than any finish does.

- **Cosmetic refresh.** Paint, a new vanity, toilet, faucets, mirror and lighting, with every fixture staying where it is. This is the least expensive option and usually the least disruptive, but it doesn't touch what's behind the tile.
- **Partial remodel.** New shower or floor tile, a new vanity, maybe a tub-to-shower swap or a glass enclosure, while most of the room stays. The risk is the wall behind the tile: if the substrate is rotted or the waterproofing has failed, a partial job turns into a gut halfway through. Our guide to a [bathtub to shower conversion in NYC](/blog/bathtub-to-shower-conversion-nyc/) covers when that happens.
- **Full gut.** Everything comes out to the studs: fixtures, tile, substrate and usually the old supply and drain lines. New waterproofing, new plumbing and electrical rough-in, new tile and fixtures go in. In older buildings, where the pipes are as old as the tile, it's usually the right call. Our [bathroom gut renovation guide](/blog/bathroom-gut-renovation-nyc/) walks through the full process.

### Can you remodel a bathroom for $5,000 in NYC?

Not with licensed professionals doing more than a light cosmetic refresh. Once plumbing, electrical, permits or building requirements are involved, the number is well above that. ## Building and borough change the math

Two identical 5x7 bathrooms can be tens of thousands of dollars apart. One sits over a clean stack in a newer condo. The other is in a prewar co-op with a board, short work hours and a drain that has to move.

- **Manhattan co-ops and condos.** Alteration agreements, board review, insurance certificates naming the building and managing agent, freight elevator reservations and restricted hours all add time, and time is labor. See how we work in [Manhattan](/areas/manhattan/).
- **Brooklyn brownstones and townhouses.** No board, but older plumbing, out-of-plumb framing and walk-up access. Opening a wall often turns up decades of earlier repairs. More on [Brooklyn](/areas/brooklyn/) projects.
- **Queens and Staten Island homes and apartments.** Often more room to work and easier access, which keeps logistics costs down, but the same licensing and permit rules apply. See [Queens](/areas/queens/) and [Staten Island](/areas/staten-island/).

## Where the money goes

Read any proposal by category: demolition and protection, plumbing, electrical, waterproofing and tile, fixtures and glass, ventilation, permits and filings, and building requirements. In New York, labor and coordination usually cost more than the tile.

That's also why a cheap-looking quote can end up expensive. When plumbing, electrical and carpentry are hired separately, each trade prices its own risk and nobody owns the gaps. A tile setter who shows up before the inspection, or an electrician who finds the vanity wall already closed, creates rework that lands on your invoice.

What moves the price most:

- **Moving the plumbing.** Relocating a toilet or shower drain means opening the floor and, in many apartment buildings, work involving the ceiling of the unit below.
- **Old pipes.** Prewar cast iron drains and galvanized supply lines are often replaced while the wall is open. It adds scope now and avoids a leak later.
- **Tile and stone.** Large-format porcelain, mosaics and natural stone take far more labor to set than the material price suggests.
- **Glass, fixtures and ventilation.** Custom glass, a steam shower, a wall-hung toilet or a properly ducted quiet fan all add line items. See our guide to [bathroom ventilation](/blog/bathroom-ventilation-installation-nyc/).
- **Electrical.** New circuits for heated floors, a bidet seat, a lit mirror or lighting controls need a licensed electrician and an electrical permit. In older homes, an overloaded panel can turn into a separate [electrical panel upgrade](/blog/electrical-panel-upgrade-nyc/).

## Costs missing from many first quotes

The number on the first page of a proposal is rarely the number you pay. Ask about these before you compare bids.

- **Asbestos.** The Department of Buildings notes that [pre-1987 buildings may have piping and equipment insulated with asbestos](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/dob/project-requirements-asbestos.page), and an asbestos assessment is required before a permit is issued. If abatement is needed, it's its own cost and its own step in the schedule.
- **Building fees and deposits.** Co-ops and condos commonly charge alteration review fees and hold a refundable damage deposit. Ask your managing agent for the alteration agreement early so these are in the budget, not a surprise.
- **Building work hours.** Many co-ops limit work to weekday daytime hours. Shorter days stretch the schedule, and that cost belongs in the quote. Our walkthrough of the [co-op and condo alteration process](/blog/nyc-co-op-condo-renovation-alteration-process/) covers what boards usually ask for.
- **Permits and filings.** Filing fees are small. The professional fees for the architect or engineer, plus the plumber's and electrician's filings, are not. A quote that doesn't mention them hasn't included them.
- **Contingency.** Rot, corroded pipes and crooked framing behind a bathroom wall are common in older buildings. Hold a reserve that isn't earmarked for upgrades.
- **Living without the bathroom.** If it's your only one, plan for a few weeks of showering elsewhere. Our guide to [living through a renovation in NYC](/blog/living-through-renovation-nyc/) covers the practical side.

## Permits and approvals that shape the price

Permits are a cost, but more importantly they decide who is allowed to do the work. According to the [NYC Department of Buildings](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/property-or-business-owner/renovating-kitchens-bathrooms.page):

- Some minor work, such as painting, plastering, installing new cabinets and resurfacing floors, can be done without a work permit.
- Most kitchen and bathroom renovations require an ALT2 permit application filed by a New York State licensed professional engineer or registered architect. The DOB's examples include adding a new bathroom, rerouting gas pipes and adding electrical outlets, and moving a load-bearing wall.
- Construction without the Department's approval or permits is illegal. The most severe violation, Class 1, can carry a $25,000 fine plus additional penalties and accrued interest, and the work must be legalized or removed.

Plumbing has its own rules. Under [Local Law 33 of 2026](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/buildings/local_laws/ll33of2026.pdf), certain "ordinary plumbing work" done by a licensed plumber doesn't need a permit, including relocating up to two fixtures within the same room by up to 10 feet, along with the related supply, waste and vent piping. Anything beyond that still needs a filing. Either way, plumbing in New York City has to be done by a licensed plumber, and electrical work by a licensed electrician.

Skipping the paperwork is the most expensive shortcut there is. Unpermitted work tends to surface at the worst time: a board inspection, a leak into the unit below, a sale or a refinance. For more on filings and timelines, see our [bathroom remodeling guide for New York](/blog/bathroom-remodeling-new-york/).

## How to build a realistic budget

1. **Define the scope in one sentence.** Refresh, partial or gut, and whether any fixture moves.
2. **Know your building.** Co-op, condo, rental or townhouse decides approvals, fees, deposits and work hours.
3. **Ask about hidden conditions.** Pipe age, past leaks and possible asbestos should be priced or carried as written allowances.
4. **Separate allowances from fixed prices.** Tile, fixtures and glass should have stated allowances so you can see what happens if you choose more expensive ones.
5. **Hold a contingency.** Set it aside before design starts, not after demolition.
6. **Check licenses and insurance.** Confirm the contractor's NYC home improvement license and that plumbing and electrical are done by licensed trades.
7. **Compare proposals line by line.** A lower total that leaves out permits, protection of common areas or inspections isn't a lower price.

## FAQ

### How much does a bathroom renovation cost in NYC?

A gut renovation with basic finishes starts at about $15,000 to $25,000. Luxury bathrooms with stone, custom glass, steam or layout changes have no real ceiling. Your building, the condition behind the walls and whether fixtures move decide where you land, so a walkthrough is the only way to get a firm number.

### Do I need a permit to renovate my bathroom in NYC?

Painting, plastering and similar minor work don't need a permit. Most bathroom renovations need an ALT2 application filed by a licensed engineer or architect, according to the DOB. Some limited plumbing work, such as moving up to two fixtures within the room by up to 10 feet, can be done by a licensed plumber without a permit under Local Law 33 of 2026.

### Is a co-op bathroom renovation more expensive than a condo?

Often, yes. Co-ops tend to have longer board reviews, stricter alteration agreements, larger deposits and tighter work hours, and shorter workdays stretch the schedule. Condos are usually simpler, and townhouses skip board approval entirely.

### What surprises should I budget for?

In older buildings, the usual ones are asbestos, corroded pipes and water-damaged framing. Building fees, deposits and the professional fees for filings also catch people off guard. Ask for these to be priced or carried as allowances before you sign.
