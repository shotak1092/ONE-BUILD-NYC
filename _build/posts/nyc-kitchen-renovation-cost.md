## Three scopes, three very different budgets

Published kitchen prices vary because people mean different things by "kitchen renovation." Before you compare any number, decide which of these you are pricing:

- **Refresh.** Paint or reface the cabinets, new hardware, a new faucet, backsplash, light fixtures. Nothing moves, no walls open.
- **Standard remodel.** New cabinets, countertops, appliances and flooring in the same layout. The sink and range stay where they are, though circuits are often added.
- **Full gut.** Walls opened, plumbing and gas relocated, new circuits, a new layout and often custom millwork.

Each step up adds licensed trades, filings and weeks to the schedule. A 70-square-foot galley in a prewar co-op on the Upper West Side and a 200-square-foot open kitchen in a Queens or Staten Island house will price very differently even with identical finishes.

Small kitchens also cost more per square foot than large ones. Permits, building deposits, a plumber's minimum visit and carrying debris down from a walk-up don't shrink with the room.

## Why a New York kitchen costs more than the national number

National cost guides are a poor yardstick here. Three things push NYC kitchens higher:

- **Licensed trades.** Plumbing and gas work has to be done by a licensed master plumber, and electrical work needs a licensed electrician and its own permit. You can't use a handyman for the parts of a kitchen that matter most.
- **Access.** Freight elevator reservations, protected hallways, building work hours and limited parking slow production. Every hour a crew waits on an elevator is billed.
- **Paperwork.** Filings, board review, insurance certificates and security deposits are part of what a New York kitchen really costs.

## Where the money goes

Cabinets, labor and appliances take most of a kitchen budget. A few published NYC reference points, from [Brick Underground's NYC renovation guide](https://www.brickunderground.com/guides/how-to-renovate):

- **Appliances.** Stock appliances run about $400 to $3,000 each. Designer and panel-ready brands run about $6,000 to well over $10,000 each. This is the line that swings the most.
- **Backsplash and counters.** Subway tile backsplash runs around $25 per square foot installed; marble, quartz and other made-to-order materials run over $100.

Because NYC kitchens are small, the slab is rarely the big number. Cutouts, edge profiles and getting stone up the stairs of a brownstone matter as much.

**Cabinets** are the other large line. Stock boxes are the cheapest; custom millwork costs more but often pays off in odd-sized prewar kitchens where standard sizes leave dead gaps.

**Ventilation** deserves its own line. In an apartment, ducting a hood to the exterior usually needs board approval, and a high-output range or induction cooktop may need more electrical capacity than the existing panel has. See our guide to [electrical panel upgrades in NYC](/blog/electrical-panel-upgrade-nyc/).

**Labor** is where unclear contracts hurt. When a general contractor, a separate plumber and a separate electrician each bill on their own schedule, the gaps between trades become idle days you pay for.

## Costs that sit outside the contractor's number

In a co-op or condo, the renovation price is only part of the check. Before you commit, budget for:

- **Alteration agreement and board fees.** Review fees, the building's architect or engineer review, and legal fees where the building passes them on.
- **A security deposit.** Refundable, but it ties up cash for the length of the job.
- **Freight elevator and supervision charges**, where the building has them.
- **Insurance certificates** naming the building and managing agent as additional insureds.
- **Drawings and filings** by a licensed architect or engineer when the work needs a permit.
- **Asbestos testing** in older buildings before demolition, where the building or the scope requires it.
- **Living without a kitchen** for the length of the job: takeout, a hot plate and storage add up.
- **Contingency.** Older buildings hide old pipes, out-of-level floors and outdated wiring. Hold a reserve for what opens up.

Each building sets its own fees and deposit. Your managing agent's alteration package is the final word. For the full approval process, see our guide to [renovating a co-op or condo in NYC](/blog/nyc-co-op-condo-renovation-alteration-process/).

## Permits and licenses that affect the price

The NYC Department of Buildings says [most kitchen and bathroom renovations need an Alteration Type 2 (ALT2) permit](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/property-or-business-owner/renovating-kitchens-bathrooms.page), the filing for work with multiple types of work that doesn't change the building's use, exits or occupancy. It must be filed by a New York State licensed professional engineer or registered architect.

Some work needs no permit. DOB lists painting, plastering, installing new cabinets and resurfacing floors as minor alterations. No permit doesn't mean no license, though: DOB says [installing new kitchen cabinets doesn't require a permit](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/property-or-business-owner/do-i-need-a-permit.page), but the contractor must hold a Home Improvement Contractor license from the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection. The same page says ordinary plumbing work, including allowable replacement or relocation of plumbing and gas piping, must be performed by a licensed master plumber.

### The 2025 Electrical Code

The [2025 NYC Electrical Code](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/buildings/bldgs_bulletins/bb_2024-008.pdf) took effect on December 21, 2025 and applies to electrical applications filed on or after that date. That matters if your kitchen adds circuits for a range, induction cooktop, under-cabinet lighting or a panel upgrade. Make sure the electrical scope in your quote is priced to the current code.

### Skipping permits costs more

DOB is blunt: construction without the Department's approval or permits is illegal. The most severe violation class can carry a $25,000 fine plus additional penalties and interest, and the work then has to be legalized or removed. For electrical work, a [June 2026 DOB advisory](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/buildings/construction_advisories/ca-elecover.pdf) lists work-without-a-permit penalties of $600 to $10,000 for one- and two-family homes and $6,000 to $15,000 for other buildings. A low bid that leaves out permits isn't a low bid.

## Schedule is part of the price

Board approval often comes before any demolition, so put the review period on the calendar first. After that, the order of trades protects the budget: demolition, plumbing and electrical rough-in, inspections, close-up, cabinets, countertops, then finishes. Buildings that end the workday mid-afternoon or ban weekend work stretch the schedule, and a longer schedule costs more labor.

## What a small budget can and can't do

A budget under about $10,000 buys a cosmetic update in New York, not a new kitchen. Realistic scopes at that level:

- Painted or refaced cabinets, new hardware and a new faucet.
- Paint, a tile backsplash and updated light fixtures.
- One or two appliance swaps in the same locations.

What it won't cover: moving the sink, adding circuits, replacing every cabinet or changing the layout. Those bring in licensed plumbing and electrical work and often a filing. If you want a kitchen that works for the next fifteen years, fund the plumbing, electrical and layout properly and phase the finishes, not the other way around.

## How to get quotes you can compare

Bids fall apart when each contractor prices a different scope. Ask everyone to price the same document:

- **Measured scope.** Dimensions, ceiling height, what stays and what goes.
- **Named allowances.** Cabinets, counters, tile, fixtures and appliances with dollar allowances, so upgrades are visible.
- **Permits and filings.** Who files, who pays, and which DOB and board approvals apply.
- **Trades.** Licensed plumbing and electrical stated explicitly, with license numbers.
- **Building logistics.** Work hours, freight, protection, insurance certificates, deposit.
- **Schedule.** Start window, rough-in and inspection milestones, finish date.
- **Change orders.** How hidden conditions are priced if the walls open up surprises.

If you want lighting control, motorized shades, built-in speakers or networked appliances, decide before the walls close. Wiring added after drywall means patching and repainting. ## FAQ

### How much does a kitchen renovation cost in NYC?

Brick Underground's NYC renovation guide puts a mid-range kitchen renovation at $25,000 to $40,000, and $90,000 and up depending on size and finishes. Scope, building rules, layout changes and appliance choices decide where your kitchen lands, so get a quote against a measured scope.

### Do I need a permit to renovate my kitchen in NYC?

It depends on the work. DOB says painting, plastering, new cabinets and resurfacing floors need no permit, though the contractor still needs a city Home Improvement Contractor license. Most kitchen renovations with multiple types of work need an ALT2 permit filed by a licensed engineer or architect, and electrical work needs its own permit.

### What costs do co-op and condo owners forget?

Board and review fees, the refundable security deposit, freight elevator and supervision charges, insurance certificates, drawings for filings and a contingency for what opens up in older walls. Your building's alteration package lists the exact amounts.

### Can I renovate a kitchen for $10,000 in NYC?

You can refresh one: painted or refaced cabinets, new hardware, light fixtures, backsplash and paint. Moving plumbing, adding circuits or replacing every cabinet needs a larger budget.
