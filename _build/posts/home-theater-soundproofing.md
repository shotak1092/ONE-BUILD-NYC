## Soundproofing and acoustics are two different jobs

A lot of people searching for home theater soundproofing end up buying foam or fabric panels. Panels don't solve the problem most of them have.

**Sound isolation** cuts how much sound passes through the room's boundary: walls, ceiling, floor and door. **Acoustic treatment** controls echo and reflections inside the room. Panels, bass traps and diffusers make dialogue clearer and the room less boomy. They do very little to stop the next apartment from hearing an action scene.

Two things to keep in mind:

- **No wall stops everything.** The goal is to bring what leaves the room down to a level that's acceptable on the other side, not silence.
- **Bass is the hard part.** Low frequencies move through the air and through the structure itself. A wall that stops voices can still pass a subwoofer.

Isolation gets built into the shell of the room. Acoustic treatment goes on top once the shell is done.

## Find the sound paths before you pick materials

Start by working out where the sound goes. In a home theater there are four paths:

- **Through the partition.** Sound passes straight through the wall, ceiling or door.
- **Through the structure.** Subwoofer energy and footsteps travel through studs, joists and slabs.
- **Through the ducts.** Supply and return ducts are tubes connecting one room to another.
- **Around the wall (flanking).** Sound skips your well-built wall by going through the floor deck, a shared ceiling or adjacent construction.

Upgrade one surface and ignore the others, and you get less than you paid for. A [Manhattan](/areas/manhattan/) prewar apartment with concrete slabs, plaster walls and old shared risers leaks differently than a wood-framed two-family in [Queens](/areas/queens/) or a [Brooklyn](/areas/brooklyn/) brownstone with joist floors. The plan has to start with the actual building.

## Walls: mass, air space and decoupling

Wall construction is where most of the measurable improvement comes from. The yardstick is STC (Sound Transmission Class), a single-number rating of how well an assembly blocks airborne sound. Higher is better.

USG's [Sound Control Manual](https://www.buildsite.com/pdf/usg/USG-Sound-Control-Manual-Sound-Construction-System-Selection-Technical-Notes-76735.pdf) lays out a clear ladder for a wood-stud wall:

- One layer of 5/8-inch gypsum board on each side tests **STC 34**.
- Doubling the layers on each side raises it to **STC 41**.
- Decoupling one side of that double-layer wall with resilient channel takes it to **STC 49**, because the channel breaks the direct path through the studs. USG puts the added cost of the channel at about 10% of the partition.

AISC's [sound isolation guide](https://www.aisc.org/globalassets/aisc/publications/facts-for-steel-buildings-4-sound-isolation-and-noise-control.pdf) gives the broader picture: single-stud walls with cavity insulation and one layer of gypsum per side typically land around STC 40 to 45, roughly STC 50 with two layers, and double-stud walls run from STC 55 to 65. It also notes that metal studs at 24 inches on center isolate mid and high frequencies better than studs at 16 inches, all else equal.

### Lab numbers versus your actual wall

Ratings come from a lab. AISC says field measurements typically come in on the order of 5 points lower. USG's manual goes further: installed performance can drop at least 5 dB, and as much as 15 dB, depending on workmanship. Plan for a margin, and hire people who detail the wall the way it was tested.

### A caution about bass

STC is a single number, and it says little about deep movie bass. If you're running a serious subwoofer, favor assemblies that decouple the structure, not just ones with a high rating on paper.

### Room within a room

Building a separate inner shell on isolators is the most effective approach, and it's the right call for a dedicated theater with a big system. It costs floor area and ceiling height and adds weight, so it needs an architect or structural engineer. In a New York apartment where every inch counts, a well-detailed double-layer, decoupled wall is often the better trade.

## Seal every gap

A wall is only as good as its weakest opening. USG's [acoustical assemblies brochure](https://www.usg.com/content/dam/USG_Marketing_Communications/canada/product_promotional_materials/finished_assets/acoustical-assemblies-brochure-en-sa200-can.pdf) says acoustical sealant can raise a double-layer steel-framed partition from STC 29 to STC 53. The Sound Control Manual has a smaller-scale example: five unsealed outlet boxes took an STC 50 partition down to STC 41.

Sealing checklist for a theater:

- **Perimeter:** acoustical sealant where drywall meets the floor, ceiling and adjoining walls.
- **Door:** a solid-core door with perimeter seals and a drop seal. A hollow-core door in a good wall undoes the wall.
- **Outlets and switches:** don't put boxes back to back in the same stud bay, and seal around each one.
- **Recessed lights:** standard cans open the ceiling. Use fixtures and enclosures that keep the ceiling layer continuous, or go with surface or cove lighting.
- **Penetrations:** every speaker wire, HDMI run and conduit that crosses the wall gets sealed.

The last three are where the trades collide. The electrician, the low-voltage installer and the carpenter all make holes in the same wall. When they work on separate contracts and separate schedules, someone cuts an opening after the sealant is already in.

## Ducts and HVAC

Ducts are the quiet leak. A duct that runs straight from the theater to a bedroom carries sound with it. Avoid a single shared run between the theater and other rooms, use longer runs with bends before the register, and keep returns from opening directly into the next space. In a high-rise or co-op, part of the HVAC may belong to the building, which brings the alteration agreement and the building's engineer in early. On a larger or more demanding room, bring in an HVAC designer and an acoustical consultant.

## Floors and ceilings: impact noise

Footsteps and dropped objects are measured with IIC (Impact Insulation Class). If there's an apartment above or below your theater, this matters as much as the walls.

In USG's wood-frame floor example, a standard assembly (wood flooring over plywood on joists, gypsum ceiling below) tests **IIC 32**. Adding carpet and pad raises it to **IIC 58**. With carpet, pad and resilient channel holding up the ceiling, it reaches **IIC 66**. Carpet and pad do little for airborne sound, but they cut impact noise dramatically.

The structure changes the answer. A National Research Council Canada [technical update on impact sound](https://www.aivc.org/sites/default/files/airbase_13023.pdf) explains that soft coverings that raise IIC a lot on concrete slabs are much less effective on wood-joist floors, where the complaint is low-frequency thumping. Joist floors need floating floors with resilient edge strips and careful detailing. If you're putting hardwood in an apartment, our guide to [apartment wood flooring in NYC](/blog/apartment-wood-flooring-nyc/) covers underlayment and building rules.

## Panels and bass traps

Once the shell is built, acoustic treatment makes the room sound right. Panels cut echo and reverberation, but they don't reduce sound passing in or out. Thin panels mainly handle mid and high frequencies, so deep bass needs thicker bass traps or dedicated corner treatment. Don't cover every surface: too much absorption makes a room sound dead. Panels go at first reflection points and in corners.

The wall behind the screen is part of the shell first: sealed, insulated and decoupled, with fabric-wrapped treatment on the surface for the in-room sound.

## Sequence the work before the walls close

This is where planning decides the result. A workable order:

1. **Set the target.** Who's on the other side of each wall, floor and ceiling, and how loud will you play?
2. **Plan the shell.** Wall type, ceiling treatment, floor build-up, door location.
3. **Plan HVAC, electrical and cable routes** before framing, so nothing gets cut through finished isolation.
4. **Rough in everything:** electrical, low-voltage, speaker wire, lighting control, shade pockets.
5. **Inspect, then insulate.**
6. **Hang drywall in layers,** with sealant, resilient channel or damping as designed.
7. **Finish and treat:** door, trim, flooring, acoustic panels, equipment.

## NYC specifics: code, permits, buildings and work hours

**The Building Code sets a floor.** [Section BC 1207 of the NYC Building Code](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/buildings/codes-pdf/cons_codes_2022/2022BC_Chapter12_InteriorEnvironmentWBwm.pdf) requires walls, partitions and floor/ceiling assemblies separating dwelling units to have an STC of at least 50 (45 if field tested), and floor/ceiling assemblies between units an IIC of at least 50 (45 if field tested). It also requires penetrations for piping, electrical devices and ducts to be sealed or treated so the assembly keeps its rating. A theater that shares a wall with a neighbor should be built well past that minimum.

**Permits.** New framing, new circuits and HVAC changes can require Department of Buildings filings and inspections, depending on scope. Adding panels to finished walls is a different job from rebuilding walls and running dedicated circuits. Confirm the scope with your contractor before work starts.

**Co-op and condo boards.** Expect the alteration agreement to call for plans, a licensed contractor and certificates of insurance. Many buildings also have rules about floor build-ups and ceiling work that affect the units above and below. See our [co-op and condo renovation](/services/co-op-condo-renovation/) page for how that process runs.

**Work hours and logistics.** Buildings limit when demolition and drilling can happen, and many require freight elevator bookings for material deliveries. Multiple layers of drywall is a lot of board to bring up. Separate trades on separate schedules stretch all of this out.

**Structure.** Extra layers and a room within a room add weight. Bring in a structural engineer where the framing or floor is in question, especially in older brownstones and townhouses.

## FAQ

### How do I soundproof a home theater on a budget?

Seal first and fix the door, because gaps and a hollow door cause the biggest losses. Then add a second drywall layer with sealant, and decouple one side with resilient channel where you have the space. In USG's tests, channel took a double-layer wood-stud wall from STC 41 to STC 49 for roughly 10% more partition cost.

### Will acoustic panels stop my neighbors from hearing the movie?

No. Panels reduce echo inside the room. They don't reduce sound going through the walls. They pair well with a properly built, sealed shell, but they don't replace it.

### Can I soundproof a room without opening the walls?

Partly. A solid-core door with seals and an extra layer of drywall over the existing wall can help. The biggest gains come from insulation, decoupling and sealing inside the wall, so a renovation is the easiest time to do it.

### Do I need a permit to soundproof a home theater in NYC?

It depends on scope. New framing, circuits or HVAC changes can require DOB filings and inspections, and co-ops and condos add their own alteration approval. Panels on existing walls usually don't. Confirm the scope with your building and a licensed contractor before work begins.

### What STC rating does NYC require between apartments?

The NYC Building Code calls for at least STC 50 (45 field tested) for walls and floor/ceiling assemblies between dwelling units, and at least IIC 50 (45 field tested) for floor/ceiling assemblies. For a home theater, aim well above that.
