## Renovation or remodel: the label doesn't decide anything

People use both words loosely. Renovation usually means updating what's there: finishes, ceilings, flooring, lighting, restrooms. Remodeling usually means changing layout or function: moving walls, adding a conference room, splitting an open floor into private offices, turning a Brooklyn retail shop into a café.

In New York, what you touch decides your path, not what you call it. Moving a partition, relocating a sink, adding circuits or changing exits each bring their own filings and inspections. So when you brief a contractor, describe the physical changes. "Eight private offices, a pantry with a sink and a server closet" tells us far more than "remodel."

## Scope the job before anyone prices it

A written scope is the cheapest protection you can buy. The GSA's [guidance on space reconfiguration and renovation](https://www.gsa.gov/governmentwide-initiatives/federal-highperformance-buildings/highperformance-building-clearinghouse/workplace-strategies/project-guidance/space-reconfiguration-renovation-and-construction) is written for federal space, but its planning list applies to any tenant: define key performance goals early, engage occupants during design, put the strategies and materials into the construction documents, and phase the work when renovating in place.

Put these on paper before you ask for bids, so every bidder prices the same job:

- **Purpose.** Fit more staff, rebrand, meet a lease obligation, replace failing systems.
- **Headcount and use.** How many people and what they do. This drives restrooms, exits and HVAC load.
- **Physical changes.** Walls, ceilings, floors, millwork, restrooms, pantries.
- **Systems.** Electrical capacity and panels, plumbing, HVAC, sprinklers, data.
- **Technology.** Lighting control, shades, conference room AV, networking, cameras and access control.
- **Building constraints.** Work hours, freight elevator access, loading rules, noise limits, and whether you stay open.
- **Budget and contingency.** Older Manhattan and Brooklyn buildings hide wiring, pipes and floor conditions behind the walls.

Technology is where most scopes fall short. Planned before the walls close, it's conduit, blocking and cable pulled while everything is open. Planned after, it's cutting into finished drywall.

## Who pays: read the lease first

For a tenant, the first budget question is who pays. The [NYC Small Business Services commercial lease guide](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/sbs/downloads/pdf/about/reports/commercial-lease-guide-accessible.pdf) says that if you need extensive alterations, you should negotiate them at the term sheet stage and decide whether you or the landlord performs the work and who pays. A common arrangement is the landlord doing the work and the tenant paying part of it. A tenant improvement allowance is the amount the landlord is willing to pay toward initial alterations.

The same guide notes you can require the landlord to review your plans in a reasonable time and negotiate a cap on any fee for reviewing plans or supervising the work. Both matter when rent is already running. We go deeper on allowances and lease timing in our post on [choosing a tenant improvement contractor in NYC](/blog/tenant-improvement-contractor-nyc/).

If you own the building or manage it, the questions flip: what base-building work you fund, what tenants may alter, which contractors are approved and which insurance certificates must be on file. Write those rules down once.

## Permits and approvals in New York City

DOB's [overview of DOB NOW for owners and managers](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/buildings/pdf/owners_presentation_rsa.pdf) is plain about it: most construction work requires a permit from the Department of Buildings, and plans are filed on the owner's behalf by a licensed Professional Engineer or Registered Architect. Electrical work must be done by a DOB-licensed electrician, and minor plumbing has its own limited alteration filing.

After the work, the same document explains:

- Certain structural, fire protection, plumbing or mechanical work needs inspections by a DOB-approved special inspector.
- Final inspection is done by DOB or certified by a registered design professional, depending on the work.
- The owner or design professional can request a Letter of Completion, or apply for a new or amended Certificate of Occupancy.

Skipping the permit is expensive. DOB says failure to obtain one results in a Work Without a Permit violation and a Stop Work Order, with significant financial penalties, and those violations are issued to the property owner.

### How big is your filing?

DOB's [alterations project category page](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/dob/project-categories-alterations.page) reserves "alteration" for work that results in a new Certificate of Occupancy: enlargements, changes of use or occupancy, and changes to exits. DOB treats these as high-risk projects that get a thorough code review. Work that doesn't change the CO is classed as a renovation. Your architect or engineer determines which applies. Confirm it before you commit to a move-in date, because the filing type shapes the timeline more than construction does.

### Building rules sit on top of city rules

Even with permits in hand, the building has its own say. In NYC, expect some or all of these:

- Landlord or managing agent approval of drawings and the contractor.
- Certificates of insurance naming the owner and managing agent as additional insureds.
- Limited work hours, with noisy work pushed to evenings or weekends.
- Freight elevator reservations and protection for lobbies and corridors.
- Advance notice for water, power or sprinkler shutdowns.

In co-ops and condos the equivalent is the alteration agreement. We cover that in our guide to [renovating a co-op or condo in NYC](/blog/nyc-co-op-condo-renovation-alteration-process/), and much of it carries over to mixed-use buildings.

## The stages of the project

Most interior projects follow the same arc, whether it's a Midtown office floor or a storefront in Queens:

| Stage | What happens | What you decide |
|---|---|---|
| Walkthrough | Site visit, existing conditions, rough scope | Goals, budget range, must-haves |
| Design | Layouts, systems, finishes, technology plan | Layout, materials, AV and lighting |
| Drawings and filing | Construction documents, DOB filing, building approval | Sign-off on drawings |
| Procurement | Pricing, long-lead items, schedule | Contract and schedule |
| Construction | Demolition, rough-in, inspections, finishes | Change orders, access |
| Closeout | Final inspections, sign-offs, punch list, handover | Acceptance, move-in |

Inside the walls, order matters more than speed: demolition, framing, plumbing, electrical and low-voltage rough-in, inspections while walls are open, insulation, drywall, finishes, fixtures, then testing. If an inspection is missed or a trade shows up late, you either wait or open finished walls. Avoiding that is the point of having one team run every trade.

## Staying open during construction

Many NYC businesses can't afford to close. The GSA guidance says to phase work when renovating in place to limit contamination between occupied and construction areas. In practice:

- **Work in zones.** Finish one area, move people, start the next.
- **Do loud work off-hours.** Demolition, core drilling and dusty work go to evenings and weekends where the building allows it.
- **Seal the boundary.** Temporary partitions, floor protection and dust control between work and occupied areas.
- **Schedule cutovers.** Water, power and network switchovers are planned ahead and done outside business hours.
- **Keep exits clear.** Occupied areas keep their exit routes, and your design professional should confirm the phasing plan does.

Phasing takes longer and usually costs more than closing outright. What you get back is revenue. Price both before deciding.

## Mistakes that cost the most

From our own projects, not a survey:

- **Signing the lease before reading the alteration clause.** Work hours, approved contractors and restoration at lease end are easier to negotiate before you sign.
- **Starting before the permit.** A Stop Work Order costs time and money.
- **Vague scope.** Bidders price different jobs, and the low number is usually the one that left the most out.
- **Hiring trades separately.** When a GC, plumber, electrician and AV installer each have their own contract, nobody owns the gaps.
- **Adding technology late.** Shades, lighting control, networking and AV are cheap to plan early and costly to retrofit.
- **No contingency.** Prewar buildings hide surprises.
- **Ignoring building logistics.** Freight elevator slots and insurance certificates have lead times. Missing them idles a crew.
- **Skipping closeout.** Letters of completion, sign-offs and warranty paperwork are easy to lose when everyone wants to move in.

## FAQ

### Who pays for renovations in a commercial lease?

The lease decides. NYC SBS advises negotiating extensive alterations at the term sheet stage, including who does the work and who pays. A landlord may offer a tenant improvement allowance toward initial alterations, or do the work and have the tenant cover part of the cost.

### Do I need a permit for a commercial interior renovation in NYC?

Most construction work does. Plans are typically filed by a licensed Professional Engineer or Registered Architect, electrical work requires a DOB-licensed electrician, and minor plumbing has its own filing. Work without a required permit brings a violation and a Stop Work Order, issued to the property owner.

### What's the difference between renovation and remodeling?

Renovation generally updates finishes and fixtures; remodeling changes layout or use. In NYC, permits and inspections depend on the actual work, not the label. DOB reserves "alteration" for work that results in a new Certificate of Occupancy.

### Can my business stay open during the renovation?

Often, with phasing. The space is split into zones, loud work goes off-hours, and construction areas are sealed off from occupied ones. It usually takes longer and costs more than closing, so compare both.

### What are the most expensive mistakes?

A vague scope, starting before permits, signing a lease without reading the alteration clause, and hiring trades separately with nobody coordinating them. Planning technology after the walls close is another common one.
