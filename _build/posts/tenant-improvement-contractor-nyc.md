## What tenant improvement means

A tenant improvement is an alteration to the interior of a commercial space to fit a tenant's use: partitions, ceilings, flooring, lighting, HVAC changes, restrooms, data and security. You'll also hear tenant build-out, fit-out or tenant finish. The label matters less than the scope. Paint and flooring in a Midtown office is one job. Moving walls, rerouting ductwork, adding restrooms or upgrading electrical service in a Brooklyn storefront is a different one, with drawings, permits and inspections attached.

### What the contractor actually does

On a typical project, the contractor:

- Walks the space and reviews the lease, work letter and building rules before pricing.
- Prices the scope and builds a schedule around your lease commencement and any rent-free period.
- Coordinates with the architect, engineers and expediter so the drawings match what can be built.
- Pulls the permits and keeps insurance certificates current with DOB and the building.
- Sequences the trades: demolition, framing, plumbing and electrical rough-in, inspections, close-up, finishes.
- Runs inspections, punch list and closeout.

On a TI, the contractor is the single point of accountability between you, the landlord and the city. That matters more than a few points of price.

## How a build-out moves from lease to opening

For a NYC space, the sequence usually looks like this:

1. **Lease and scope.** Before signing, decide what you need to build and what the landlord will fund or allow. A walkthrough can flag problems like undersized electrical service, no path for kitchen exhaust or too few restrooms.
2. **Design and drawings.** A registered design professional prepares the documents DOB will review. When a lease clock is running, this stage gets compressed.
3. **Landlord approval.** The lease and building rules decide what must be submitted for approval and which insurance certificates are required.
4. **Permits and plan review.** Review time depends on scope and filing type. Budget real time for it.
5. **Construction.** The schedule is driven by scope, building access, work hours and material lead times. Get a sequenced schedule in writing rather than a generic range.
6. **Inspections and closeout.** Final inspections, sign-offs and, where the scope requires it, a new or amended certificate of occupancy.

The stretch between "construction done" and "open for business" is the part tenants underestimate most. Inspections, sign-offs and systems testing all have to line up. Build your buffer at the end, not just the start.

## Who pays for tenant improvements

The lease decides. The city's [commercial leasing guide from NYC Small Business Services](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/sbs/downloads/pdf/about/reports/commercial-lease-guide-accessible.pdf) says that if you make the alterations, the landlord may provide a tenant improvement allowance to cover some or all of the costs. Another common setup is the landlord doing the work and the tenant paying part of it. Allowances are typically quoted per square foot. Three practical points:

- **Allowances rarely cover everything.** Get a contractor's number before you accept the allowance, so you know the gap you'll fund.
- **Check what the allowance can be spent on.** Some leases exclude furniture, equipment, cabling or signage. Tell your contractor what falls outside it.
- **Ask for rent abatement.** The SBS guide notes that landlords will often agree to rent abatement when you need time to build, and that it's best raised at the term sheet stage. If the build-out takes months, you don't want to pay rent on an empty space the whole time.

The same guide recommends consulting professionals from the start, because they can help you ask the right questions and negotiate a lease that protects you. An early contractor walkthrough is cheap insurance against signing for a space you can't afford to build.

## NYC requirements that shape every build-out

### A licensed general contractor pulls the permit

DOB says [work permits are only issued to a Licensed General Contractor](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/dob/project-requirements-contractor-permit-and-insurance.page) with a New York City business address. DOB checks the contractor's insurance automatically when the permit application goes in: general liability of at least $1 million per occurrence, plus workers' compensation and disability insurance. Electrical work needs a [Licensed Master Electrician and an electrical permit](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/property-or-business-owner/project-requirements-owner-electrical.page), and the SBS guide notes landlords often require licensed contractors for electrical, gas and plumbing alterations.

A handyman or a friend of the property manager isn't an option for permitted work. Ask every bidder which licensed GC will pull the permit and to show you the license and insurance, not just describe them.

### Tenant protection in occupied buildings

Most NYC build-outs happen in occupied buildings. Under the NYC Construction Codes, [construction documents for alterations in an occupied building must include a tenant protection plan](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/industry/project-requirements-registrant-alt-multi-dwelling-n-site-req.page) with specific provisions for egress, fire safety, structural safety, health requirements and noise restrictions. Everyone engaged in the work has to act reasonably to keep the site safe. Your contractor should be able to explain how dust, noise and corridor access will be handled for the neighbors on your floor.

### Special inspections

Depending on scope, the owner has to [hire a special inspection agency before the permit is issued](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/industry/project-checklists-registrants-alt-cmu.page). Structural work, fire protection and some mechanical items are common triggers. Your engineer or architect of record will confirm what applies.

### Landlord approval

The SBS guide says most landlords let tenants make cosmetic or decorative alterations without approval, but will always require review and approval of alterations that need a permit or affect building systems. Get that approval in writing before demolition. Don't rely on a verbal okay from a building manager.

### Building rules: hours, insurance and access

Every building sets its own rules on work hours, freight elevator reservations, loading dock use, protection of common areas and the insurance certificates it wants naming the landlord and managing agent. Ask for the alteration rules and contractor requirements during lease negotiation. Many Manhattan office buildings push noisy demolition to after hours, and that changes the price. In mixed-use co-op or condo buildings, the board's alteration process adds its own steps, which we cover in our guide to the [co-op and condo alteration process](/blog/nyc-co-op-condo-renovation-alteration-process/).

### Permit fees

DOB fees vary with the scope of work, and an expediter's fees sit on top. Ignore fixed figures from national articles. Ask your contractor and expediter for an estimate for your scope and building.

## How to vet a tenant improvement contractor

| What to check | What good looks like |
|---|---|
| License | A NYC Licensed General Contractor who will pull the DOB permit, with license details shown to you |
| Insurance | Policies on file with DOB, plus certificates naming the landlord and managing agent as the building requires |
| Trades | Licensed plumbing and electrical, in-house or under one contract |
| Building experience | Past work in occupied NYC buildings with freight elevators, work-hour limits and tenant protection plans |
| Scope clarity | A written scope that separates what the allowance covers from what you pay |
| Schedule | A sequenced plan tied to lease commencement and rent abatement, with inspections built in |
| Closeout | A plan for punch list, sign-offs and any certificate of occupancy work |

A license from another state doesn't carry over. The permit has to come from a contractor licensed with NYC DOB.

### Questions to ask at the first meeting

- Who is my single point of contact from walkthrough to closeout?
- Which trades do you employ or schedule yourself, and which do I hire separately?
- Who handles the landlord and managing agent paperwork?
- What happens to the schedule if plan review runs long?
- What is excluded from your price?

Specific answers beat polished ones. A contractor who says "it depends on your building" and then tells you exactly what it depends on is the one to keep talking to.

## Why one contract for all trades matters

A commercial interior touches several trades at once. Walls can't close until plumbing and electrical rough-in pass inspection. Data, audio-visual and lighting control wiring has to go in before drywall. When each trade has its own contract, someone has to referee the sequence, and that someone is you.

A design-build contractor with construction, plumbing, electrical and low-voltage under one roof takes that off your plate. One schedule, one insurance package for the building, one person to call when an inspection date moves. Landlords and managing agents get one party to coordinate with instead of four.

## A short pre-lease checklist

Before you sign, run through these with your contractor and attorney:

- Get the building's alteration rules and contractor insurance requirements in writing.
- Confirm what the landlord's work letter covers and what the allowance can be spent on.
- Get a rough scope and cost range for your layout, so the allowance gap isn't a surprise.
- Negotiate rent abatement that matches a realistic construction and inspection schedule.
- Confirm electrical capacity, restroom count and mechanical systems suit your use.
- Ask which work needs the landlord's written approval.

Your attorney should review the lease itself. A contractor can tell you what the work will take; the legal terms are your lawyer's job.

## FAQ

### What does a tenant improvement contractor do?

A tenant improvement contractor builds out or alters the interior of a leased commercial space to fit the tenant's use. That includes construction, coordinating plumbing, electrical and mechanical work, handling permits and inspections, and managing the schedule through closeout. In NYC, the DOB work permit is issued only to a Licensed General Contractor.

### Who pays for tenant improvements, the landlord or the tenant?

The lease decides. Landlords often offer a tenant improvement allowance that covers some or all of the cost, and the tenant typically pays the rest. Review the lease and work letter with your attorney before signing.

### How long does a commercial tenant improvement take in NYC?

It depends on scope, plan review, building rules and inspections. A simple refresh can move quickly; a build-out with new restrooms or electrical service takes months. Ask your contractor for a sequenced schedule tied to your lease commencement date.

### Do I need landlord approval before starting work?

For anything beyond cosmetic changes, yes. NYC's commercial leasing guide says landlords will always require review and approval of alterations that need a permit or affect building systems. Get it in writing before demolition.
