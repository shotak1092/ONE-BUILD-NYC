## What a tub to shower conversion involves

There are two different jobs that go by this name. A full conversion removes the tub and builds a real shower. A cutout modifies the existing tub so it's easier to step into. Most NYC homeowners who ask about replacing a bathtub with a walk-in shower mean the first, and it follows a set sequence:

1. **Protect and demolish.** The tub, surround tile and usually the drywall or plaster behind it come out. In a prewar building, this is where you learn what the walls and floor really look like.
2. **Plumbing rough-in.** A shower drain sits in a different spot than a tub drain, and the shower needs a new valve. Supply lines and venting are adjusted to suit.
3. **Framing and substrate.** Blocking goes in for grab bars, the floor is sloped to the drain and the wall backing goes up.
4. **Waterproofing.** A continuous membrane across the floor, curb and walls is what keeps water out of the ceiling below. You can never inspect it once the tile is on.
5. **Tile, glass and trim.** Tile is set, grouted and sealed. Glass and fixtures follow.
6. **Inspection and closeout.** Where a permit applies, inspections happen at set points, and in a co-op or condo the work is signed off with the managing agent.

Each step depends on the one before it. If tile goes up before the rough-in is inspected, or the valve is chosen after the wall is closed, you pay to open it again.

## Where conversions go wrong

Most problems come from the same few places:

- **Waterproofing treated as a finish.** Tile isn't waterproof and neither is grout. The membrane behind and under the tile is the barrier, and it has to be built and tested as part of the pan.
- **Drain and slope shortcuts.** A floor that doesn't pitch correctly holds water against the curb and grout lines.
- **Picking the valve late.** The valve body lives inside the wall. Choosing a pressure-balancing or thermostatic valve after the walls close means reopening finished work. Decide on a handheld, rain head or body sprays before rough-in.
- **No plan for the unit below.** In a multifamily building, a leak is your neighbor's ceiling and a conversation with the board, not just your repair.
- **Skipped paperwork.** The NYC Department of Buildings says plainly on its [kitchen and bathroom renovation page](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/property-or-business-owner/renovating-kitchens-bathrooms.page) that construction without its approval or permits is illegal, and that a Class 1 violation, the most severe kind, can carry a $25,000 fine plus additional penalties and interest.

## Permits and approvals in NYC

This is where New York differs most from the generic advice you'll find online.

### What the DOB expects

The DOB's [replacement installations list for water plumbing](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/buildings/pdf/replacement_installations-water_plumbing.pdf) puts a residential shower and a bathtub in Category 2 under the Administrative Code. The same DOB renovation page says permits may be needed depending on how complex the work is, and that most kitchen and bathroom renovations require an ALT2 application filed by a professional engineer or registered architect.

In practice, the more the job changes the plumbing, the more likely a filing is needed. Moving the drain, rerouting supply lines or reworking the floor is a different scope from setting a new fixture in the same spot. We sort this out at the walkthrough, before any price is final.

### A Licensed Master Plumber does the plumbing

The DOB's [owner guide to plumbing work](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/property-or-business-owner/project-requirements-owner-plumbing.page) states that the plumbing work it covers can only be performed by a Licensed Master Plumber. It also notes that replacing an existing plumbing fixture can be minor work done without a work permit. A conversion that relocates the drain and valve goes beyond that. It's one more reason to have the plumber on the same contract as the builder, instead of a separate hire you have to manage.

### Inspections set the schedule

The DOB's [plumbing inspections tool kit](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/buildings/pdf/tool_kit_plumbing_inspections.pdf) says that at the roughing inspection, the tub or shower body and pressure-balancing valve piping must be completed, and stall showers need their venting and traps in place. Tub connections need a separate inspection if they weren't inspected during roughing. The rule for your schedule: no tile and no closed walls until the rough plumbing has passed.

### Co-op and condo approval

In a co-op or condo, DOB rules are only half the process. The board or managing agent will typically want an alteration agreement, drawings or a scope of work, proof of the contractor's license and insurance, a certificate of insurance naming the building, and a schedule that fits the building's work hours. Many Manhattan and Brooklyn buildings also require you to book the freight elevator for demolition and deliveries. Every building's rules differ, so read the alteration policy before you sign anything.

## Full replacement or tub cutout?

A cutout, sometimes called a step-through, removes a section of the tub wall so you don't have to lift your leg over the rim. A full replacement removes the tub and builds a new shower. These questions usually settle it:

| Question | Points toward a cutout | Points toward full replacement |
|---|---|---|
| What do you want to change? | Easier entry only | Layout, tile, glass, fixtures, floor |
| What shape is the tub in? | Sound, finish is acceptable | Leaks, soft walls, dated tile |
| Want a bench, niche or body sprays? | No | Yes |
| Wheelchair, walker or future mobility? | A low step is enough | Curbless or low-threshold shower |
| Resale or rental goals | Quick, limited upgrade | A bathroom that reads as new |

For most of the homeowners, investors and property managers we work with, a full replacement is the right call because it renews the waterproofing, the valve and the drain at the same time. A bathroom you'll live with for 15 years deserves a pan and valve built to last that long.

## Planning for comfort and safety

A lot of people convert because they want a safer, easier shower, whether for aging in place or just daily use. Build that in now, because adding it later means opening finished walls.

- **Blocking for grab bars.** Solid backing between the studs costs little during framing and lets you add bars anywhere, any time.
- **Hand shower.** A handheld on a slide bar makes seated showering, cleaning and bathing kids easier. The valve and supply have to be planned for it.
- **Low threshold, slip-resistant floor.** Smaller floor tile means more grout lines and more grip. A low curb or curbless entry changes the floor framing and slope, so decide before rough-in.
- **Bench and niche.** Both are framed into the wall during construction. Easy to include now, hard to add cleanly later.

## Budget and timeline

Cost depends on five things: whether the drain moves, the size and type of shower pan, tile and glass choices, what's behind the tub once it's out, and what the building requires for approvals, insurance and work hours. A swap that keeps the plumbing wall in place is a different project from one that moves the drain or reframes the floor. Prewar buildings in Manhattan, Brooklyn and Queens tend to reveal surprises once the tub is out, and co-op or condo conditions add steps.

If the conversion turns into a full gut of the bathroom, a gut remodel with basic finishes in NYC starts at roughly $15,000 to $25,000, and luxury bathrooms have no real ceiling. For a conversion on its own, we price from a walkthrough of your bathroom, not a generic number. If budget is the main constraint, our guide to a [bathroom remodel on a budget](/blog/bathroom-remodel-on-a-budget-nyc/) shows where to spend and where to hold back.

On timing, demolition, rough-in, waterproofing cure, tile and glass run in sequence over a few weeks, and the inspection and any board sign-off set the pace. What delays these jobs is rarely the tile. It's a rough-in that fails inspection, glass measured too early, an approval still pending or a building that limits noisy work to certain hours. Ordering glass and valve trim early and getting approvals before demolition keeps the schedule intact.

Demolition and tile cutting also leave fine dust that travels well beyond the bathroom. Plan for a proper final cleaning once the work is done, on top of the protection and daily cleanup during the job.

## FAQ

### How much does it cost to convert a bathtub to a shower in NYC?

It depends on whether the drain moves, the pan type, tile and glass, the condition of the walls and what your building requires. Filing and design fees add to construction when a permit is needed, and co-op or condo conditions can add more. If it becomes a full bathroom gut, expect to start around $15,000 to $25,000 with basic finishes. We quote conversions from a walkthrough.

### Do I need a permit to replace a tub with a shower in NYC?

Often, yes. The DOB lists residential showers and bathtubs as Category 2 replacement installations, and it says most bathroom renovations require an ALT2 filed by an engineer or architect. Swapping a fixture in place can be minor work, but moving the drain and valve is not. The plumbing must be done by a Licensed Master Plumber.

### Can I convert a tub to a shower in a co-op or condo?

Usually, with board approval. Most buildings require an alteration agreement, a licensed and insured contractor, a certificate of insurance naming the building and work within set hours. Start with your building's alteration policy and your managing agent.

### What should I add for older adults or limited mobility?

Blocking in the walls for grab bars, a hand shower on a slide bar, a low or curbless threshold, a bench and slip-resistant floor tile. Put the blocking in during the conversion even if nobody needs bars yet.
