## What the 2026 benchmarks say

There's no single price for an office renovation, but two large commercial real estate firms publish annual fit-out benchmarks that are useful for sanity-checking a budget. Read them carefully, because they measure different things.

- **Cushman & Wakefield** puts [New York City fit-out construction at $212.59 per square foot](https://www.cushmanwakefield.com/en/united-states/insights/office-fit-out-cost-guide) in its 2026 guide, among the most expensive U.S. markets. Its published construction figures are limited to physical construction. They don't include low-voltage cabling, A/V, security, furniture or soft costs.
- **JLL** puts the average medium-quality corporate fit-out across 50 U.S. and Canadian markets at [$295 per square foot, with a typical range of $230 to $375](https://www.jll.com/en-us/guides/us-canada-office-fit-out-costs-guide). That's a regional average, not a New York number. JLL notes that New York anchors the high end of the cost range.

These are broker benchmarks for corporate fit-outs, not contractor quotes and not One Build NYC pricing. A small office in a Queens or Staten Island building, a cosmetic refresh of a few rooms, or a full gut of a Midtown floor will all land in very different places. Use the benchmarks to test whether a proposal is in the right range, then get an itemized number for your space.

## Refresh, remodel or full gut

Scope sets the budget more than any finish does.

| Scope | What it usually means | Where it lands |
|---|---|---|
| Refresh | Paint, flooring, lighting, small partition changes | Lowest; scales with finishes |
| Remodel | New layout, partitions, ceilings, electrical and data rework | Closer to the construction benchmarks |
| Full gut | Strip to shell, new mechanical, electrical and plumbing, new everything | Top of the range |

Layout matters as much as finish level. JLL's 2026 guide ranges from about $255 per square foot for a baseline open layout to $355 for a high-spec traditional layout with more enclosed offices. Every private office adds framing, a door, finishes, lighting, and often its own HVAC and electrical work.

## What New York adds

Labor rates explain part of the premium. The rest comes from how buildings here operate:

- **Work hours.** Many office buildings restrict noisy work to nights and weekends. Off-hours labor costs more, and your lease or the building's alteration rules set the hours.
- **Landlord approvals.** Commercial landlords usually require an alteration agreement, stamped plans, and certificates of insurance naming the owner and managing agent before work starts.
- **DOB filings.** Work that changes layout, plumbing, electrical or egress generally needs a filing with the NYC Department of Buildings, prepared by a licensed architect or engineer.
- **Asbestos.** The DOB notes that [pre-1987 buildings may have piping and equipment insulated with asbestos](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/dob/project-requirements-asbestos.page), and an asbestos assessment is required before a permit is issued. In older Manhattan and Brooklyn buildings, plan for it.
- **Freight and logistics.** Freight elevator reservations, loading dock windows and protection of common areas all take time, and time is labor.

## Where the money goes: Cushman & Wakefield's New York example

Cushman & Wakefield's 2026 guide includes a worked all-in example for New York City. Its hard-cost table totals $220.62 per square foot, including general conditions, fee and contingency. The two largest trade lines are mechanical, plumbing and fire protection at $50.19 and electrical at $46.25. It then adds percentages of hard cost for everything else:

| Line | Per sq ft | On 5,000 sq ft |
|---|---|---|
| Hard construction | $220.62 | $1,103,100 |
| Soft costs, 10% (design, filings, fees) | $22.06 | $110,300 |
| IT, 4% | $8.82 | $44,100 |
| A/V, 12% | $26.47 | $132,350 |
| Furniture, 22% | $48.54 | $242,700 |
| Miscellaneous, 2% | $4.41 | $22,050 |
| **All-in** | **$330.92** | **$1,654,600** |

The 5,000-square-foot column is our math: the guide's per-square-foot rates multiplied out and rounded. Treat it as a benchmark for a corporate fit-out, not a quote for your floor.

Two takeaways:

1. **Furniture, A/V and IT add up to about a quarter of the all-in total**, and they're often missing from the first conversation with a contractor.
2. **The biggest construction lines are mechanical, plumbing and electrical.** Those are exactly the trades that have to be coordinated, because a ceiling can't close until ducts, pipes, wiring and sprinklers are in, inspected and signed off.

## What's in an estimate, and what usually isn't

Sort every number into three buckets so you can compare proposals line by line.

- **Hard costs:** demolition, framing, drywall, ceilings, flooring, doors, millwork, plumbing, electrical, HVAC modifications, fire protection and finishes.
- **Soft costs:** architect and engineer fees, expediting, DOB filings, insurance and landlord review fees.
- **Vendor and move-in costs:** data cabling, security, A/V, signage, furniture, the move itself and the final clean.

If one proposal includes cabling and filings and another doesn't, the cheaper one isn't cheaper. Also check whether your lease includes a tenant improvement allowance, and what it's allowed to pay for.

## Why prices keep moving

JLL's 2026 guide names three forces pushing fit-out costs up: tariffs on construction materials, tight specialty-trade labor, and energy and supply-chain disruption. It finds security, IT and A/V make up about 10% to 12% of fit-out cost, with a median increase of 8% year over year. Cushman & Wakefield reports U.S. fit-out costs up about 5% year over year.

What that means in practice: a quote that sits for months loses value. Lock scope early, order long-lead items such as switchgear, lighting and glass partitions once the design is fixed, and ask how long a price holds.

## How to set the contingency

A renovation has unknowns that new construction doesn't: old piping, hidden water damage, outdated wiring, asbestos behind walls. Writing in Facilities Net, engineer James Piper notes that [for most projects a 10% to 15% contingency provides adequate protection](https://www.facilitiesnet.com/commercialofficefacilities/article.aspx?id=18667), with the right size depending on the project's complexity and the age of the building.

Older New York buildings sit at the higher end of that. Keep the contingency separate from the base budget and spend it only on documented conditions, not upgrades.

The most common source of late surprises is technology. When A/V, networking, lighting control and shades are specified after the walls close, they get chased through finished work. Plan them at design stage.

## Ways to keep the number down

1. **Decide quality tier and layout first.** They move the price more than any later choice.
2. **Reuse what works.** Existing ceilings, HVAC distribution and furniture in good shape cut both hard and vendor costs. Spend on client-facing space first.
3. **Settle scope before pricing.** Changes after demolition cost more than changes on paper.
4. **Sequence the trades.** Rough plumbing and electrical, inspections, then close walls. A missed inspection idles the whole crew.
5. **Handle building rules early.** Work hours, elevator reservations and insurance certificates need to be arranged before mobilization.
6. **Get one itemized proposal.** If plumbing, electrical and low-voltage arrive as three separate bids, you're the one combining them and finding the gaps.

## Is there a "30% rule" for renovations?

People ask about it, but it isn't a building code or DOB standard. If the question is tax treatment, whether costs are deductible repairs or capitalized improvements, the IRS covers that in its [tangible property regulations](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/tangible-property-final-regulations), and it's a question for your CPA before the scope is final.

## FAQ

### How much does it cost to renovate an office in New York?

Cushman & Wakefield's 2026 guide puts New York City fit-out construction at $212.59 per square foot, and its all-in New York example, with soft costs, IT, A/V and furniture, at $330.92. Those are corporate fit-out benchmarks. A refresh costs far less, and your number depends on layout, finishes, building rules and how much existing infrastructure stays.

### What's the most expensive part of an office renovation?

Construction is the largest share, and within it mechanical, plumbing, fire protection and electrical lead. Furniture is usually the next biggest item, followed by A/V.

### Does the construction price include furniture and cabling?

Usually not. Cushman & Wakefield's construction figures exclude low-voltage cabling, A/V, security, furniture and soft costs. Ask every bidder to list what's included so you can compare like with like.

### How much contingency should I hold?

For most renovation projects, 10% to 15% is a common guideline, and older buildings with unknown conditions justify the higher end. Hold it separately and release it only for documented conditions.
