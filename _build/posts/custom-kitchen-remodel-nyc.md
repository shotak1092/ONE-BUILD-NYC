## What a custom kitchen remodel actually includes

"Custom" gets used loosely. In practice it means decisions are made for your room and your habits, not picked from a fixed package: cabinets sized to your walls, a layout that may move the sink or range, specific counter materials, and lighting and power planned around how you work.

The National Association of Home Builders lists remodeling goals that include [upgrading cabinets, counters, appliances and fixtures](https://www.nahb.org/other/consumer-resources/thinking-about-home-remodeling-start-here) and creating a floor plan customized for your lifestyle. That's a good working definition: start from how you live, then work back to materials.

A typical custom scope has these parts:

- **Layout and design.** Measured drawings, a zone plan, and a decision on whether walls, windows or doorways move.
- **Cabinetry and storage.** Custom or semi-custom boxes, pull-outs, a pantry system, appliance garages, and scribe and trim for out-of-square walls.
- **Surfaces.** Counters, backsplash, flooring and paint.
- **Mechanical systems.** Plumbing and gas lines, outlets, circuits, ventilation and sometimes a panel upgrade.
- **Lighting and controls.** Task, ambient and accent layers, dimming, and sometimes smart controls and motorized shades.
- **Appliances.** Chosen early, because cutout sizes, venting and power needs drive the rough-in.

## Remodel, renovation or refacing: which one do you need?

These terms overlap, but the work is different. Forbes Home describes a remodel as [a large project that drastically transforms the space](https://www.forbes.com/home-improvement/kitchen/kitchen-remodel-cost/), which may mean reworking the floor plan, taking out walls, relocating plumbing or upgrading electrical. A renovation restores the kitchen or makes it look new: refinishing cabinets, swapping faucets, fixing flooring.

Cabinet refacing sits at the light end. This Old House explains that refacing replaces the doors and drawer fronts and applies new veneer to the existing cabinet frames, and that it [typically takes three to five days](https://www.thisoldhouse.com/kitchens/how-to-reface-cabinets), while a full renovation can leave the kitchen unusable for weeks. It makes sense when the boxes are sound and you like the layout. It can't fix a bad layout or damaged cabinets.

| Your situation | Likely path |
|---|---|
| Layout works, boxes are solid, finishes are dated | Refacing or refinishing, new counters and hardware |
| Layout works, but storage, lighting or appliances don't | Partial remodel with new cabinetry and updated electrical |
| The layout frustrates you daily, or you want to open a wall | Full custom remodel with design, permits and relocated utilities |
| Walls, floors or systems are failing behind the finishes | Gut renovation, with the kitchen as part of a bigger scope |

## Plan the layout around how you cook

Good kitchens are designed from habits outward. Before you look at door styles, spend a week noticing how the room gets used. NAHB advises writing a prioritized list of needs and wants and thinking through traffic, furniture placement, lighting and how the space will be used. For a kitchen, that turns into questions:

- Who cooks, and is it often two people at once?
- Where do groceries land when you walk in, and how far is that from the fridge?
- Do you bake, entertain, or mostly prep and reheat?
- What do you own that has no proper home: a stand mixer, sheet pans, small appliances?
- Does anyone sit in the kitchen to work or eat?

### Storage is about location, not count

Homes & Gardens puts it plainly: it's [less about how many cabinets you have and more about their location](https://www.homesandgardens.com/kitchens/how-to-design-a-kitchen). Pots near the range, knives and boards near the prep surface, plates near the dishwasher. In a small Manhattan or Brooklyn apartment kitchen, deep drawers, vertical tray dividers and uppers that run to the ceiling usually beat adding another wall of cabinets.

### Light the kitchen in layers

Plan task lighting for prep and cooking, ambient light for the room, and accent light for depth. Timing matters here. Under-cabinet lighting, dimmers and any control system need wiring before the drywall closes. ### Decide appliances early

A 36-inch range, a panel-ready refrigerator or a big hood changes cabinet dimensions, gas and electrical needs, and sometimes the permit scope. In apartment buildings, hood venting is often limited by what the building allows, so confirm the venting path before you pick the hood. Choose appliances before cabinets are ordered, not after.

## NYC permits, licensed trades and what the DOB expects

This is what separates a New York kitchen from a generic one. The NYC Department of Buildings says [most kitchen and bathroom renovations require an ALT2 permit application](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/property-or-business-owner/renovating-kitchens-bathrooms.page), filed by a licensed Professional Engineer or Registered Architect. Its examples include rerouting gas pipes, adding electrical outlets and moving a load-bearing wall.

The same page lists minor work that may be done without a work permit, including painting, plastering, installing new cabinets and resurfacing floors. Contractors doing home improvement work still must be licensed by the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection (DCWP). So a cabinet swap and a layout change are very different projects on paper.

Plumbing and gas are their own track. The DOB notes that even work treated as ordinary maintenance and repair, including allowable replacement and relocation of plumbing and gas piping, [must be done by a licensed Master Plumber](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/property-or-business-owner/project-requirements-owner-mechanical.page). Moving a sink, adding a pot filler or relocating a gas line isn't handyman work. Our [licensed plumbing](/services/plumbing/) and [electrical](/services/electrical/) trades work under the same contract as the carpentry, so inspections are scheduled in sequence instead of chased across companies. ### Co-ops and condos

In a co-op or condo, the DOB is only one approval. The building usually requires an alteration agreement, drawings reviewed by its engineer or managing agent, certificates of insurance naming the building, and rules on work hours, freight elevator use and debris removal. Boards can ask for changes and review takes time, so submit early. Our walkthrough of the [co-op and condo alteration process](/blog/nyc-co-op-condo-renovation-alteration-process/) covers the steps. We handle the insurance certificates and work within the building's hours, so the board and managing agent deal with one contact.

### Brownstones and houses

In a Brooklyn brownstone or a house in Queens or Staten Island there's usually no board, but older buildings bring their own surprises: undersized or ungrounded wiring, old supply and drain lines, out-of-level floors and plaster walls. Budget time and money for what opens up behind the finishes.

## The usual sequence and how long it takes

This Old House outlines the general order: demolition, structural work, electrical and plumbing rough-ins, insulation and drywall, cabinet and countertop installation, then finishing touches like paint and hardware. It also notes that [thorough planning can take three to six months](https://www.thisoldhouse.com/kitchens/the-don-t-get-burned-remodeling-guide) between final design and a finished kitchen.

In practice the calendar splits into phases:

1. **Design and selections.** Measurements, drawings, appliance and cabinet choices. Custom cabinetry has lead time, so this phase sets the pace.
2. **Approvals.** DOB filing where required, plus the co-op or condo board package.
3. **Demolition and rough-in.** Walls open and utilities move. Plumbing and electrical rough-ins get inspected before anything is closed.
4. **Close-up.** Insulation, drywall, and plaster or skim coat.
5. **Cabinets, counters and fixtures.** Counters are templated after cabinets are set, which creates a gap you should expect.
6. **Finishes and punch list.** Paint, hardware, backsplash, final connections and touch-ups.

Multi-trade projects slip at the hand-offs. If the electrician shows up after the drywall, or the plumber waits on a cabinet that hasn't shipped, the schedule stalls. With one project lead scheduling every trade, you have one schedule and one weekly conversation.

## What drives the cost of a custom kitchen

Two kitchens of the same size in New York can differ in cost by multiples. The main drivers:

- **Scope.** Keeping the layout versus relocating plumbing, gas and walls.
- **Cabinetry.** Fully custom, semi-custom or stock, plus interior fittings.
- **Surfaces and appliances.** The biggest swing in material cost.
- **Building conditions.** Old risers, outdated wiring, elevator limits, floor protection and restricted hours all add labor in NYC.
- **Permits and professional fees.** Filings, drawings and board requirements.

National averages rarely fit a New York apartment, because they blend very different markets and scopes, and NYC labor and building requirements run higher. Ask any contractor for an itemized proposal built from your room, not a per-square-foot guess, and hold a contingency for what opens up behind the walls, especially in prewar buildings.

## Plan the walls before you pick the tile

Most kitchen regrets aren't about the countertop. They're about things that could have been solved before the walls closed: no outlet where the mixer lives, a hood that can't vent where the drawing says, a dimmer that needs a neutral nobody ran, a shade pocket nobody framed.

## How to hire a kitchen contractor in New York

Check the license first. Home improvement contractors in NYC must be licensed by DCWP, and you can look them up with DCWP's license check. Our NYC Home Improvement Contractor license is #621290. Then ask:

- Who files the permit, and which licensed professional signs the drawings?
- Who holds the plumbing and electrical licenses, and are they on the same contract or separate companies?
- Will I get an itemized proposal listing labor and materials before work starts?
- How are delays and changes handled? Every change should be a written change order.
- Do you know my building? Ask about alteration agreements, insurance certificates and work-hour limits.
- How do you communicate? A single contact, regular site visits and a shared log beat scattered texts.

## FAQ

### Is a custom kitchen remodel the same as a renovation?

Not quite. A remodel changes the space, which can include layout, walls, plumbing and electrical. A renovation restores or refreshes what's there, like refinishing cabinets or replacing fixtures. People use the terms interchangeably, so define your scope in writing. A clear scope is what lets a contractor price it and tells you whether permits apply.

### Do I need a permit to remodel my kitchen in NYC?

Often, yes. The DOB says most kitchen renovations need an ALT2 application filed by a licensed PE or RA, for example when rerouting gas, adding outlets or moving a load-bearing wall. Installing new cabinets alone may not need a work permit. In a co-op or condo you'll also need the building's approval. Your contractor and filing professional should confirm the path for your exact scope.

### How long does a custom kitchen take?

Plan for months, not weeks. This Old House puts final design to finished kitchen at three to six months, and custom cabinet lead times and co-op or condo board review can push that further. Construction is only part of the calendar.

### Can I keep my cabinets and still get a new look?

Sometimes. If the boxes are sound and the layout works, refacing with new doors and drawer fronts changes the look in days with less disruption. It won't fix a poor layout or damaged cabinets, so a walkthrough is the best way to judge.

### What should be in the contract?

An itemized list of labor and materials, a schedule, how delays are handled, and a written change order for every change. Also confirm who handles permits, building paperwork and insurance certificates.
