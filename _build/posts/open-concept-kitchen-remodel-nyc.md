## What an open concept kitchen remodel changes

An open concept kitchen removes some or all of the wall, peninsula or upper cabinets that separate the cooking area from the living or dining room.

In a Manhattan prewar apartment or a Brooklyn brownstone, the reason is usually practical. A galley kitchen behind a door can feel like a closet. Opening it brings window light across the whole floor and keeps the cook part of the room.

There are three common versions:

- **Full removal.** The wall comes out entirely, and kitchen, dining and living become one room.
- **Partial opening.** A wide pass-through or a large cased opening keeps some wall for storage, a refrigerator or a hood.
- **Peninsula or island conversion.** A half wall or peninsula is replaced by an island with seating, which keeps a visual boundary without blocking sight lines.

In a co-op, the partial option is often the right call. It can cut the structural and mechanical work and still change how the room feels.

## Step 1: Find out what the wall is doing

Before sketching an island, you need to know whether the wall is load-bearing, meaning it carries weight from the floors or roof above. Common clues: the wall runs across the floor joists rather than parallel to them, or it lines up with a wall or beam on the floor above or below. Clues are not proof. A structural engineer traces the framing and puts the answer in writing.

If the wall is load-bearing, it gets replaced by a beam carried on posts, with temporary shoring while the wall comes down and stamped drawings behind it. In a multi-story building, those posts have to land on something that can carry them, which is a bigger question in an older New York building than it sounds.

A non-structural wall isn't automatically simple. Kitchen walls often hold:

- Plumbing supply, drain and vent lines, sometimes a shared riser
- Gas piping to the range
- Electrical circuits, switches and outlets
- Ductwork or a bathroom exhaust running to the roof

Each of those has to be rerouted, and in this city that is licensed work. That's why the walkthrough comes before the design is fixed: the open plan has to fit what is actually inside the wall.

## Step 2: Permits and approvals in New York City

Most apartment kitchen projects have two separate layers of approval.

**The city.** The NYC Department of Buildings says that several kinds of alteration work, including piping, fuel-burning equipment, ductwork, electric wiring and the repair or replacement of finishes, can only be performed by contractors with specialized licenses. Electrical work requires a separately submitted electrical permit and a New York City licensed electrical contractor, and plumbing and gas piping work goes to a Licensed Master Plumber ([NYC DOB](https://www.nyc.gov/site/buildings/property-or-business-owner/project-requirements-owner-alt-residential.page)). Removing a structural wall adds engineered drawings to the filing.

**Your building.** In a co-op or condo, the work also has to be approved under the building's alteration agreement. Boards typically want plans, a properly licensed contractor and a certificate of insurance naming the building and managing agent, and they set work hours and freight elevator rules. Many won't review a request without drawings that show what's coming out.

The two tracks depend on each other. The board wants to see the plans the DOB will see, and the DOB filing should match what the board approved. Change the layout midway and both sets of paperwork need updating. We handle DOB permits, co-op and condo alteration requirements, building insurance certificates and limited work hours as part of the project, so you have one contact instead of an architect, an engineer and three trades.

**Landmarks.** If the building is an individual landmark or sits in a historic district, as many brownstone blocks do, check with the Landmarks Preservation Commission too. LPC lists "any interior work that requires a permit from the Department of Buildings" among the work that needs an LPC permit, along with interior work that affects the exterior, such as new vents ([NYC LPC](https://www.nyc.gov/site/lpc/applications/permit-types.page)). LPC staff decide which type of permit the job needs after reviewing the application, so file early.

New York also has its own building codes, so a detail that's standard elsewhere may not be accepted here. For more on the filing sequence, see our guide to [kitchen remodeling in New York: permits, costs and timeline](/blog/kitchen-remodeling-new-york/).

## Step 3: Design the layout for how you actually cook

Once you know what can move, design around clearances, not around a photo. The National Kitchen & Bath Association (NKBA) guidelines, as summarized by Fine Homebuilding, give the numbers that matter most ([Fine Homebuilding](https://www.finehomebuilding.com/project-guides/kitchens/kitchen-islands-practical-makes-perfect)):

| Space | NKBA recommended minimum |
|---|---|
| Work aisle, one cook | 42 inches |
| Work aisle, two or more cooks | 48 inches |
| Walkway around an island | 36 inches |
| Counter edge to wall, seating with no traffic behind | 32 inches |

These are guidelines, not code. The same source works through a modest 3-by-5-foot island and finds it needs about 120 square feet of floor once you add the space around it. In many NYC apartments that rules out a large island, and that's fine. A smaller island or a peninsula with seating usually works better than a big one that pinches every aisle.

Two more checks from the same guidance:

- **Work triangle.** No leg between sink, range and refrigerator shorter than 4 feet or longer than 9 feet, and nothing, an island included, in the way of any leg.
- **Island depth.** Anything narrower than standard 24-inch cabinets won't take a sink, cooktop or dishwasher. Go much wider than about 5 feet and items get stranded in the middle.

If the island will hold a sink or cooktop, say so at the first design meeting. A sink in an island needs a drain and vent path through the floor, and a cooktop needs a ventilation plan. Both are far easier to solve before the subfloor is open than after the cabinets arrive. In an apartment building, the drain path below your floor may also run into the neighbor's ceiling, which brings the board back in.

### Replace the storage the wall used to provide

This is the part that surprises people. The wall usually carried base cabinets below and wall cabinets above, so you lose storage on both sides. Measure the linear feet of base and upper cabinets on that wall, then decide where each foot goes:

- Full-height pantry or tall cabinets on a wall you keep
- A deeper island with storage on both faces
- Drawers instead of doors in the base cabinets you keep
- A pantry closet or a freestanding unit nearby

In an open plan, everything in the kitchen is visible from the living room, so cabinet and appliance finishes matter more. Panel-ready appliances and consistent door styles help the kitchen read as furniture.

### Noise, smells and ventilation

Without a wall, cooking noise and odors reach the whole room. Plan a hood that vents outside rather than recirculating where the building allows it, a quiet dishwasher, and soft surfaces in the living area. Many co-ops limit venting through the facade or shared shafts, and in a landmarked building a new exterior vent is an LPC item, so settle it during the alteration review.

## Step 4: Plan lighting and technology before the ceiling closes

An open kitchen loses the walls that held switches and defined zones, so light does that job. A practical scheme layers three types:

- **Task lighting** at prep areas, usually under-cabinet LED strips
- **Ambient lighting** overhead, often recessed fixtures spaced to avoid shadows over the sink and cooktop
- **Accent lighting**, such as pendants sized to the island

Layering lets you run the kitchen bright while cooking and dim it when the same room is used for dinner or a movie. That calls for dimmers or a control system, and the wiring has to be in the walls and ceiling before drywall. ## What does an open concept kitchen remodel cost?

It depends on what's in the wall, how long the opening is and what the kitchen itself includes. Price the wall as its own line, separate from cabinets, counters and appliances, because it swings the most.

Cost drivers, from smallest to largest:

1. **Non-structural wall, nothing inside.** Demolition, patching, flooring transitions and paint.
2. **Non-structural wall with mechanicals.** Add licensed plumbing and electrical rerouting and the permits that go with them.
3. **Load-bearing wall.** Add engineering, stamped drawings, a beam, temporary support and finishing.
4. **Building requirements.** Limited work hours, common-area protection, freight elevator scheduling and insurance paperwork all add labor time in a co-op or condo.

Because no two walls are alike, we price after the walkthrough, not from a formula. Our [NYC kitchen renovation cost guide](/blog/nyc-kitchen-renovation-cost/) covers what drives the kitchen side of the number.

## Who should be on the job

An open kitchen touches construction, plumbing and electrical, and often low-voltage work for lighting control and audio. When those are four companies, someone has to coordinate rough-in, inspections and finishes in the right order, and a delay in one trade holds up the rest.

A sensible sequence:

1. Walkthrough and measurements
2. Wall investigation and engineer's determination
3. Design and clearance check
4. Board package, DOB filing and, if needed, LPC
5. Demolition, shoring and structural work
6. Rough plumbing, electrical and low-voltage
7. Inspections, then drywall, cabinets, counters and finishes
8. Final inspection and building sign-off

Skipping or reordering these steps is where projects stall, usually at inspections.

## FAQ

### Can I remove a kitchen wall in a New York apartment?

Often, yes, but not on your own say-so. Much of the work needs licensed trades and DOB permits, a co-op or condo board has to approve the plan under the alteration agreement, and a structural wall needs engineered drawings. In a landmarked building or historic district, LPC signs off too.

### How do I know if my kitchen wall is load-bearing?

A wall that runs across the joists or lines up with a wall or beam above or below is a candidate. Those are only clues. A structural engineer traces the framing and gives a written determination, which you'll need for the filing anyway.

### Is an open concept kitchen worth it in a small NYC apartment?

For many small apartments, yes. Removing or widening a wall shares window light and makes a tight kitchen feel larger. A partial opening or a peninsula can deliver most of the benefit with less structural work, and clear aisles of 36 to 48 inches matter more than island size.

### How long does an open kitchen remodel take?

It depends on whether the wall is structural, how long board and DOB approvals take, and the building's work hours. The paperwork comes before demolition, so it's part of the timeline. We confirm a schedule after the walkthrough and wall investigation.
