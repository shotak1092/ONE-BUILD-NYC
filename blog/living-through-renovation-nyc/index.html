Living Through a Renovation in NYC: A Practical Guide

How to live through a renovation in an NYC apartment or house: when to stay or move out, temporary kitchens, dust, noise and building rules.

You can live through most renovations, but how well it goes is decided before demolition, not during it. The households that cope best split the home into a living zone and a work zone, set up a temporary kitchen and bathroom early, and know the building's rules and the schedule before day one. In New York, where a co-op or condo board, the building's work hours and several licensed trades all have to line up, the simplest way to keep daily life working is one contractor team and one point of contact running every trade.

Should you stay or move out?

It depends on the scope. Painting, flooring in one room and a single bathroom remodel (when you have a second bathroom) are usually livable. A kitchen remodel is possible with a good temporary setup, but expect several weeks without a working kitchen. A whole-home gut renovation usually means moving out, at least for the heaviest stretch.

In a New York apartment, the deciding question is whether you can actually separate a living area from the work area. A one-bedroom where the only bathroom and the kitchen are both open to the studs leaves nowhere to retreat to.

A quick self-check:

  • Can you keep one bathroom and one sleeping space completely untouched?
  • Can you make basic meals without the kitchen, and for how many weeks?
  • Will water or power be shut off for a full day or more?
  • Does anyone at home have a reason to avoid dust, such as an infant, a pregnancy or asthma?
  • Does the plan touch more than half of the home?

Two or more problem answers point toward a short-term rental or a stay with family for the loudest, dustiest phase.

Get the home ready before demolition

Most of the work of a live-in renovation is front-loaded.

Take stock and clear out. HGTV suggests listing your belongings and their values for insurance purposes, then sorting them into donate, sell, hand-me-down and save piles (HGTV). Anything you won't need until the job is done should leave before demo, whether to a spare room or a storage unit. In Manhattan a storage unit is often the only realistic option, so book it early.

Keep only essentials unpacked. For a kitchen job, that can be one place setting per person. For a bathroom job, a shower caddy.

Put the schedule on paper. AARP recommends marking the dates you won't have access to certain areas and when water or power will be turned off, and keeping forms, receipts and warranties together (AARP). In a co-op or condo, add the alteration agreement and the building's insurance certificate requirements to that folder.

Leave room in the budget. AARP also advises padding the budget for surprises. In prewar buildings and brownstones, old pipes, old wiring and out-of-level floors are where the surprises usually are.

Plan who moves what. A renovation crew will protect floors and shift an appliance out of the way, but packing and moving your belongings is a separate job. Sort it out before demo day.

Set up a temporary kitchen and bathroom

The temporary kitchen

Fine Homebuilding lists a microwave, a hot plate, a toaster oven, a crock pot, an electric kettle and paper plates or compostable utensils as a workable kit, with the refrigerator kept running behind a clear barrier from the work area. It also warns never to use a propane or charcoal grill indoors (Fine Homebuilding). In a city apartment, that means electric appliances only.

Add disposable plates and cups so you aren't washing dishes in a bathroom sink, frozen meals, bottled water and a separate takeout budget.

Check your circuits before you plug everything in. A microwave, kettle and toaster oven on one old circuit in a prewar apartment will trip it. That's one reason our electrical and construction crews plan the job together. If the kitchen is the project, decide the kitchen lighting while the ceiling is open; adding circuits after it's closed costs more.

The bathroom

Keep at least one bathroom working. If a home has two or more, renovate them one at a time. If you only have one, ask for a sequence that limits its downtime, and line up somewhere else to shower for the days it's out of service.

Dust, noise, kids and pets

Contain the dust

No method stops dust completely. Fine Homebuilding says there is no way to truly keep it inside the work area, even with magnetic flap doors, vent blocks and good plastic walls. The goal is to reduce it and keep it away from where you sleep and eat:

  • Zipper doors or plastic barriers sealed at the ceiling and floor between work and living areas
  • Closed or blocked vents in the work zone
  • Floor protection along the paths the crew uses
  • Covered furniture in rooms next to the work
  • Daily cleanup by the crew, and daily dusting on your side of the barrier

Older buildings and lead paint

Much of New York's housing was built before 1978. In pre-1978 homes, firms doing renovation work that disturbs paint must be EPA- or state-certified (EPA) and must give occupants EPA's Renovate Right lead hazard pamphlet before work starts (EPA). New York City adds its own lead-safe work practice requirements, including dust control and daily cleanup (NYC HPD). Ask about it at the first walkthrough, and keep children, pregnant family members and pets out of the work area.

Noise and routines

Expect noise through most of the workday, with demolition and tile cutting the loudest phases. Your contractor should warn you before shutting off water, power or heat. If you work from home, plan a coworking day pass or a library for those phases.

Build slack into the end date. Back-ordered materials and inspection timing can push a finish out by weeks, so don't schedule a holiday dinner or a move-in the week after the planned finish.

Children, pets and older relatives

Agree on off-limits rooms and a quiet homework spot on the far side of the home. Plan for pets on demolition and delivery days. For older family members, keep a clear walking path free of cords, transition strips and floor-protection edges.

NYC rules that shape daily life

Living through a renovation in New York has a layer most national guides skip: the building and the city.

Work hours. The city's construction noise rules allow work between 7 AM and 6 PM on weekdays; work outside those hours, including weekends, needs after-hours authorization from the Department of Buildings (NYC DEP). Co-op and condo alteration agreements are often stricter, with shorter weekday windows and separate limits on noisy work. Fewer work hours means a longer project, so factor your building's rules into the timeline.

Board approval. In co-ops and most condos, work needs the board's sign-off on plans before it starts. Our guide to the co-op and condo alteration process walks through it.

Permits and licensed trades. In New York City, plumbing work must be performed by a Licensed Master Plumber and electrical work by a city-licensed electrical contractor (NYC DOB). Many jobs need DOB filings, and inspections create pauses when a room sits half-finished. Ask for those pauses on the schedule up front.

Insurance certificates. Buildings require the contractor's insurance certificates before work begins. Missing paperwork can stall a start date.

Freight elevators and deliveries. In an apartment building, the freight elevator, the service entrance and the super's booking windows control when materials come in and debris goes out. In a Brooklyn brownstone or a Staten Island house, it's the dumpster and the curb. Plan deliveries around them.

Why one team makes living in easier

Most of the daily strain of a live-in renovation comes from sequencing: the plumber finishes, then the electrician needs the wall reopened, then the inspector can't come until Thursday. Every handoff adds days of dust and uncertainty.

At One Build NYC, general construction, plumbing, electrical and low-voltage work run under one contract and one schedule. Rough-in, inspections and finishes happen in order, the board deals with one contact and one insurance package, and lighting control, shades, audio and networking are planned before the walls close, so nobody opens finished drywall to run a wire.

Our advice on staying put: decide it at the first walkthrough, with the whole scope on the table. A kitchen or a single bathroom, with a clear split between work zone and living zone, is something many New Yorkers handle well. A gut renovation, or a job that cuts water and power for days, is usually easier on your household if you step out for the loudest phase.

FAQ

Can you live in your home while renovating?

Often yes, for painting, single-room flooring, one bathroom when a second is available, and kitchens with a temporary setup. Whole-home gut renovations, jobs that shut off water or power for days, and households with an infant, a pregnancy or someone with breathing problems are the cases where moving out for the worst stretch makes sense.

How do you keep dust out of the rest of the home?

Seal the work area with zipper doors or plastic barriers at ceiling and floor, close vents in the work zone, protect floors along walking paths and clean daily. Expect some dust to get through anyway.

What do you eat during a kitchen remodel?

Set up a microwave, hot plate or toaster oven, kettle and refrigerator in another room, use disposable dishes, and stock frozen meals and bottled water. Budget for takeout and cook with electric appliances only.

What hours can contractors work in NYC apartments?

City rules allow construction from 7 AM to 6 PM on weekdays, with after-hours work needing DOB authorization. Many co-ops and condos set shorter hours in their alteration agreements, so check your building's rules before you set the schedule.
