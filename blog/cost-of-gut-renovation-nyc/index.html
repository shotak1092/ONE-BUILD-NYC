Cost of a Gut Renovation in NYC: A Budget Guide

What a gut renovation costs in NYC per square foot, what the estimate leaves out, and how to budget for permits, co-op fees and contingency.

Brick Underground's NYC renovation guide puts a gut renovation with mid-range materials at $250 to $300 per square foot, and well above that for high-end finishes, structural work or landmarked buildings. For a 1,000 sq ft apartment, that's roughly $250,000 to $300,000 before soft costs. Those are published market averages, not a quote. This guide shows how to turn the per-foot number into a real budget for your apartment or house, what the price includes, what sits outside it, and what pushes it up.

What a gut renovation means

A gut renovation strips a space to the studs and subfloor and rebuilds it. Walls, floors, ceilings, the kitchen, the bathrooms and, in most cases, the plumbing and electrical systems are replaced or rerouted. A cosmetic update touches finishes and fixtures. A gut touches everything behind them.

That's why the per-foot figure is so much higher. You pay for demolition and haul-away, new rough plumbing and wiring, inspections, drywall, tile, cabinetry, flooring, paint, and the coordination of every trade in the right order. In an NYC building you also pay for logistics: protecting common areas, carrying material through freight elevators or up stairs, and working inside the hours your building allows.

NYC gut renovation cost per square foot

Finish level and layout change the result more than anything else. Brick Underground's NYC guide gives these averages:

Level Published range per sq ft What it usually means
Basic renovation, stock materials $100 to $200 (guts at the higher end) Labor and stock materials, not always a full gut
Gut with mid-range materials $250 to $300 New systems, quality but not custom finishes
Higher-end renovation $200 to $400 Better materials, custom work
Extreme cases Up to $800 The top of the market

When you compare quotes, confirm the number is for a full gut with new plumbing and electrical, not a refresh. A low per-foot figure often describes a different job.

Treat any published range as a starting point. The guide is undated, and a contractor can only price your project after a walkthrough, because the building, the floor, the access and the existing conditions are all part of the price.

What 1,000, 1,500 and 2,000 sq ft can cost

This table is simple arithmetic on the mid-range average. It shows scale. It isn't a quote.

Size At $250 per sq ft At $300 per sq ft
1,000 sq ft $250,000 $300,000
1,500 sq ft $375,000 $450,000
2,000 sq ft $500,000 $600,000

Brick Underground's larger examples show how fast scope moves the total:

  • A 2,500 sq ft prewar apartment: about $625,000 to $750,000, and double that for top-of-the-line upgrades.
  • A 3,200 sq ft brownstone: about $800,000 to $960,000, closer to $1.6 million with major structural changes and top finishes, and $2 million or more in a historic district.

A few notes on reading these totals:

  • Bathrooms and kitchens drive the number as much as square footage. Wet rooms cost far more per foot than bedrooms, so a three-bath apartment costs more than a same-size apartment with one.
  • Houses carry extra scope. A brownstone in Brooklyn, or a house in Queens, Staten Island or Westchester, can include roof, exterior and structural work that apartments don't.
  • Individual pieces have their own floor. On our own jobs, a bathroom gut remodel with basic finishes runs at least $15,000 to $25,000, and luxury bathrooms have no real ceiling. A standard electrical panel replacement runs $1,500 to $3,000, and a 100 to 200 amp upgrade runs $3,000 to $5,000.

What's included, and what's budgeted separately

A construction estimate and a total project budget are different documents.

Typically in the construction price:

  • Demolition, debris removal and site protection
  • Framing, drywall, plaster and insulation
  • Rough and finish plumbing
  • Rough and finish electrical, including panel work where the scope calls for it
  • Tile, flooring, cabinetry, countertops, doors, trim and paint
  • Project management and trade scheduling

Often separate:

  • Architect, engineer or expediter fees
  • DOB filing and permit fees
  • Co-op or condo review fees, deposits and insurance costs
  • Furniture, rugs and art
  • Appliances, depending on who supplies them
  • Window treatments, lighting control and audio-visual systems if they aren't in the contract

Ask every contractor to state in writing which of these their number covers. Two quotes that look far apart can differ only in what they leave out.

What pushes the cost up or down

Existing conditions

What's inside the walls matters more than what's on them. Old galvanized or cast-iron pipe, outdated wiring, uneven floors, water damage and out-of-plumb walls all add labor. Prewar buildings tend to surprise more often than newer ones.

Asbestos is the NYC-specific one. The Department of Buildings notes that pre-1987 buildings may have piping and equipment insulated with asbestos, and it requires an asbestos assessment before a permit is issued. A Certified Asbestos Investigator determines whether the work is an asbestos project. If it isn't, an ACP-5 report goes to the Department of Environmental Protection. If it is, abatement becomes its own cost and its own step in the schedule.

Layout changes

Keeping the kitchen where it is and the bathrooms over existing drain lines is usually the biggest saving available. Moving a kitchen, combining two bathrooms or relocating risers means new rough plumbing, more floor work and often a harder board review. Opening a wall between a kitchen and living room can be simple or expensive, depending on what the wall carries.

Material and finish quality

This is the part you control most directly. A budget tile and a premium tile take about the same labor to install, so the difference is all material. The same goes for plumbing fixtures, stock cabinets versus custom millwork, and flooring species.

Timing of the quote

Prices move. Ask how long a quote is held and how material price changes are handled in the contract.

Building rules and access

A walk-up with no elevator costs more to build in than a building with a dedicated freight elevator. The city allows residential construction on weekdays, 7 AM to 6 PM, and many co-ops and condos set tighter limits. Shorter days mean a longer schedule, which costs more in labor and in your carrying costs.

NYC permits, filings and the DOB process

A gut renovation almost always needs a DOB filing. In DOB NOW, work that doesn't affect the Certificate of Occupancy is filed as an Alteration, which replaced the old Alt-2 and Alt-3 types. Work that does affect it is filed as an Alteration CO, the replacement for Alt-1, and costs more to prepare.

In practice you will need:

  • Drawings and a scope of work from a registered design professional where DOB requires them
  • A DOB filing, with plumbing and electrical done by licensed trades
  • Inspections at the right stages, which is why rough-in sequencing matters
  • Sign-offs at the end, so the work is closed out properly

Permit and professional fees are separate from construction. They vary by job, so ask your architect or expediter for actual figures for your building.

Co-op and condo costs most budgets miss

If you own in a co-op or condo, the building is effectively your first regulator. The alteration agreement spells out what you may change, what the board needs, what insurance contractors must carry, when work may happen and what you owe the building.

Expect these lines:

  • Review fees. Brick Underground puts plan review by the board's architect at around $1,000 to $2,000, with ongoing oversight at another $8,000 to $12,000 where the building requires it.
  • Deposits. Buildings set their own amounts, so get the figure from your alteration package.
  • Insurance certificates. CityRealty notes that most condos and co-ops require at least $1,000,000 in contractor coverage. Check your own package for the exact limits and additional insured wording.
  • Elevator, loading and protection requirements. These can add time and cost to every delivery.
  • Time. Budget the months before construction as part of the project. Since February 2026, a board representative must also attest in DOB NOW that the board authorized a unit owner's or shareholder's filing.

How much contingency to hold

A gut opens spaces nobody has seen in decades. Keep a reserve on top of the contract price, and keep it separate from upgrades. Brick Underground advises budgeting at least 15 percent over the original estimate, plus a change-order line of 5 to 10 percent. Older buildings with more opened walls and floors call for the larger end. We'll talk through the right figure for your building at the walkthrough.

What the reserve is for:

  • Hidden conditions, such as deteriorated pipe, damaged subfloor or non-compliant wiring
  • Asbestos or lead handling if testing calls for it
  • Approved change orders, in writing

What it isn't for: new finish upgrades chosen mid-project. Price those as change orders and decide them deliberately.

Ways to control the budget

  • Decide before demolition. Finalize layout, fixtures, tile, cabinetry and lighting before work starts. Changes after the walls close cost the most.
  • Keep wet walls where they are. Reusing plumbing locations saves labor and filing complexity.
  • Spend where you touch. Hardware, faucets, doors and countertops get used daily. Economize on surfaces nobody notices.
  • Plan technology early. Lighting control, shades, audio and networking go in far more easily with the walls open. See our smart home and lighting control pages.
  • Get like-for-like quotes. Have each contractor price the same drawings and allowances, and list inclusions and exclusions.
  • Ask about scheduling. When plumbing, electrical, carpentry and low-voltage come from different companies, gaps between trades add weeks.

FAQ

How much does it cost to gut a 2,000 sq ft house in NYC?

At Brick Underground's mid-range average of $250 to $300 per square foot, about $500,000 to $600,000 in construction cost. High-end finishes, structural work, exterior scope or layout changes move it higher, and architect fees, permits and furnishings come on top.

How much does it cost to gut a 1,500 sq ft home?

The same range works out to about $375,000 to $450,000 for construction. Bathrooms, kitchen quality and existing conditions are the biggest variables. Only a walkthrough turns that range into a firm number.

Is it cheaper to gut a house or rebuild it?

In New York, a gut is often the only practical choice, especially in apartments, attached houses and landmarked buildings. Rebuilding adds foundation, structure, exterior and zoning questions. Which costs less depends on the structure's condition and what your lot allows, and an architect can confirm both.

What is the 30% rule for renovations?

There's no official rule. People use the phrase for different thresholds, such as capping renovation spending at a share of the home's value. For a gut, a written budget with a separate contingency line matched to your building's age is more useful.

Does a gut renovation estimate include permits and co-op fees?

Usually not. Construction estimates cover the building work, while architect fees, DOB filing costs, board review fees, deposits and furnishings are typically separate. Ask your contractor to list what is and isn't included.
