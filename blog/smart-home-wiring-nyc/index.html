Smart Home Wiring in NYC: What to Run and When

Smart home wiring for NYC renovations: which cables to run, what to plan before drywall, and how city low-voltage rules and building approvals apply.

Smart home wiring is the cabling behind your network, lighting control, audio, video and sensors, and the time to install it is while the walls are open. Run Cat6A to every spot that might need a connection, bring every cable back to one central location, label both ends and leave conduit where you're unsure. In New York City the work also has to fit city low-voltage rules, your building's alteration process and its work hours, so it gets sequenced with the rest of the renovation.

What a smart home actually needs

A smart home isn't one wire. It's several cable types, each with a job:

Cable What it does Typical places
Cat6A Ethernet Network, power over Ethernet (PoE), control Access points, cameras, TVs, keypads, home office
Coax Antenna or cable-service feeds TV locations, the incoming service point
Fiber Long runs, very high bandwidth Large townhouses, backbone runs between floors
HDMI / AV cable Video to displays and projectors Media rooms, living room and bedroom TVs
Low-voltage control cable Keypads, sensors, shade and lighting control Wall keypads, door and window sensors
Speaker cable Built-in and home theater audio Ceiling and in-wall speakers

For most Manhattan apartments and Brooklyn brownstones, Ethernet is the center of the system. It carries the internet, powers many devices over PoE and keeps the controller, cameras and access points off a crowded Wi-Fi band. CEDIA, the home technology trade association, says in its renovation guidance that Cat6A or a higher standard is necessary for all data cabling in a smart home.

Do you need neutral wires at the switches?

In most living spaces, yes. Many smart switches and dimmers draw a small, constant current, so they need a neutral (grounded conductor) in the switch box. The National Electrical Code added that requirement to give electronic lighting controls such as occupancy sensors a complete circuit path, as EC&M explains. According to IAEI Magazine, the rule covers switch locations where general-purpose branch circuits serve bathrooms, hallways, stairways and habitable rooms, while a closet is not considered habitable space.

When we open a wall, we pull neutrals to every switch location that will carry a smart control, whether or not the code strictly requires it there. Our lighting control planning guide shows how that fits into a full lighting design.

Wired or wireless: what to hardwire

Neither wins everywhere. In an apartment that stays closed up, wireless devices are usually the faster, cheaper answer. In a home being gutted to the studs, hardwiring the core of the system is worth it, because a cable doesn't drop a signal or need a battery.

A sensible split for a renovation:

Hardwire

  • The network backbone: every Ethernet drop, the access points and the central rack.
  • Wall keypads and lighting control.
  • Ceiling and in-wall speakers.
  • Displays, projectors and everything in a media room.
  • Fixed cameras and video intercoms that support PoE.
  • Power and control for motorized shades, planned before ceilings and window pockets close.

Wireless or battery is fine

  • Door, window and leak sensors.
  • Plug-in smart outlets and lamps.
  • Temperature and humidity sensors in rooms you didn't plan for.

The controller is only one piece. CEDIA's guide to choosing a controller says the network, wiring, connected products, programming and everyday controls all have to work together, and that construction materials, interference and equipment placement affect performance. Prewar plaster, masonry walls and steel in older NYC buildings are hard on Wi-Fi. A hardwired access point in each zone fixes most coverage problems at the source.

How to plan the wiring in a renovation

Plan the system before the walls close. CEDIA notes that cable routing is cheaper at first fix and can get expensive in a retrofit, and that cabling done at the build stage usually ends up invisible.

  1. Walk the space room by room. Mark where each TV, speaker, keypad, shade, camera, access point and sensor will go, and where the furniture will sit, since that sets outlet and drop heights.
  2. Pick the central location. CEDIA says the hub location should be decided before work starts, because every cable connects back to it. In an apartment that's usually a closet, a utility niche or a spot near the entry. Give it ventilation and its own circuit.
  3. Run more than you think you need. CEDIA advises running cable across the property even where you're not sure a room will need it. An extra drop costs little with open walls and a lot afterward.
  4. Add conduit where the future is unclear. Conduit in walls and under floors gives future cables a path without opening anything up.
  5. Label both ends. Each cable should be marked with its type and destination at both ends, so it can be serviced or upgraded later.
  6. Sequence with the other trades. Low-voltage rough-in happens alongside electrical and plumbing rough-in, before insulation and drywall, and before the inspection that lets the walls close.

Rooms that need extra thought

  • Bedrooms: a box on each side of the bed with outlets and USB, plus an Ethernet drop for the TV.
  • Kitchens: appliance circuits, under-cabinet lighting control, a keypad at the entry and a display location if you want one. See kitchen remodeling.
  • Living rooms and media rooms: HDMI or Ethernet to the display, speaker cable to each surround position and a clean path to the rack. See our guide to media room installation in NYC and our home theater page.
  • Windows: power and control for motorized shades, placed before ceilings and pockets are finished.
  • Home office: at least two Ethernet drops, plus one for a printer or network storage.

NYC permits, licensed trades and building rules

Three layers apply in New York City, and each one affects the schedule.

City law. Local Law 128 of 2024 added §28-105.4.7 to the Administrative Code. It says an electrical permit isn't required for wiring designed to run under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits, provided the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person as the NYC Electrical Code defines it. Some low-voltage work still has to be done by a licensed master or special electrician, including wiring tied into life safety systems such as fire alarms, and wiring that connects to a control circuit that switches light, heat or power circuits. That second category matters for smart homes, since lighting and shade control often do exactly that.

Line-voltage work is electrical work. Switches, neutrals, new circuits, dimmer panels, shade power supplies and panel changes need a licensed electrician and, in most cases, a DOB electrical permit. If the plan needs more capacity, read our guide to electrical panel upgrades in NYC. Rules change, so confirm the current DOB requirements for your scope before work starts.

Your building. Co-ops and condos usually require an alteration agreement or board approval before work that opens walls, along with insurance certificates, set work hours and freight elevator bookings. Include the wiring scope in the renovation package; boards often want low-voltage runs inside walls described too. Our guide to the co-op and condo alteration process covers what to expect.

Your schedule. Building work hours limit how much rough-in fits into a day. In a Manhattan high-rise that can mean weekday hours only and booked elevator time; in a Brooklyn or Queens brownstone, it means fishing cable through old framing and plaster.

Why the wiring needs one team

Smart home wiring is where the trades collide. The electrician sets boxes, the low-voltage installer pulls cable, the plumber runs pipe through the same walls and the carpenter wants to close up. When four companies handle those steps, things get missed: a switch box without a neutral, a drop hidden behind a cabinet, a conduit with no pull string.

The fix is to treat the wiring plan as part of the design. We plan lighting control, shades, audio and networking at the first walkthrough, before demolition is done. Our licensed trades, general construction, plumbing, electrical and low-voltage, work under one contract, one schedule and one project lead, so rough-in, inspections and finishes happen in the right order and the board deals with one contact and one insurance package.

What it costs

Smart home wiring is priced by scope: the number of drops, the size of the home, wall and ceiling construction, how many rooms get audio or video, and whether the walls are already open. Timing matters most. Wiring done as part of a gut renovation or a kitchen and bath remodel is almost always the most efficient version of the job.

For a useful quote, bring:

  • A list of rooms and devices you want, even if it's tentative.
  • Your building's alteration requirements, if you're in a co-op or condo.
  • Floor plans, or photos of possible rack locations.
  • Your timeline and the building's work hours.

FAQ

What wiring does a smart home need?

At minimum, Cat6A Ethernet to key locations, low-voltage control cable for keypads and sensors, speaker cable for built-in audio and HDMI or Ethernet for displays. Add coax at TV and service locations, and neutrals at every switch that will carry a smart control.

Can I add smart home wiring after the walls are closed?

Yes, but it costs more and usually means patching. Wireless devices fill many gaps, and conduit installed earlier gives new cable a path. If you're renovating anyway, wire before the drywall goes up.

Do I need a permit for smart home wiring in NYC?

Under §28-105.4.7 of the Administrative Code, qualifying wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data doesn't need an electrical permit, but it must be done by a licensed master or special electrician or a qualified person. Work tied to life safety systems, or to controls that switch lighting or power circuits, must be done by a licensed electrician. Line-voltage work is electrical work and generally needs a permit. Your co-op or condo board will have its own approval process.

Is hardwired or wireless better for home automation?

In a gut renovation, hardwire the backbone: network, access points, keypads, speakers and displays. Wireless works well for sensors and for rooms that stay closed up. Most good systems use both.
