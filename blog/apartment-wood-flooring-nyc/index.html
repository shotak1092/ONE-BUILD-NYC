Apartment Wood Flooring in NYC: Solid vs. Engineered, Costs, Rules

Solid or engineered wood for a NYC apartment? Slab and subfloor needs, co-op and condo board rules, noise, cost drivers and care.

In a New York City apartment, the right wood floor is the one your slab, your building and the neighbors below will accept. Often that's engineered wood rather than solid. Solid planks can be sanded many times, but over a concrete slab they need a plywood subfloor. Engineered boards can often go down directly. Before taste comes into it, the alteration agreement and its sound requirements usually decide the question. This guide covers the product choice, slab prep, board rules, noise, cost drivers and the order the job runs in.

Solid vs. engineered wood

The National Wood Flooring Association describes solid wood as one piece of wood from top to bottom, usable in any room on or above ground and refinishable many times. Engineered wood is real wood built in multiple layers with a high-quality wood top layer. It's more dimensionally stable than solid and can be refinished, though not as many times.

Consumer Reports adds that engineered flooring is a thin veneer of real wood or bamboo over structural plywood. It handles higher humidity and moves less than solid wood. How often you can refinish it depends on the product: some engineered floors can be refinished once or twice, and some not at all. Ask before you buy.

Solid wood Engineered wood
Construction One piece of wood Real-wood top layer over plywood layers
Movement with humidity More Less
Refinishing Many times Depends on the wear layer: once or twice, sometimes never
Typical apartment install Fastened over a plywood subfloor Often glued to a flat, dry slab
Effect on floor height Higher, because of the subfloor Lower

The last two rows come from how NYC apartment buildings are built. Many apartments, especially in postwar and high-rise buildings in Manhattan, Brooklyn and Queens, sit on concrete slabs. Brownstones and many older prewar buildings have wood framing underneath instead, which changes the prep. Engineered boards can be glued to a flat, dry slab. Solid boards need a plywood subfloor fastened over it, which raises the floor and adds days. That shows up at door thresholds, under the dishwasher and at the bottom of the entry door.

Which one to pick

Choose solid when you have the ceiling height, the building allows a fastened subfloor, and you want the longest refinishing life. Consumer Reports notes that solid wood dents easily and expands and contracts with humidity, so match the species and finish to how you live.

Choose engineered when the slab is your starting point, when humidity swings in the unit, or when you want wood with less seasonal movement.

If you're renovating the whole apartment, make this call before the kitchen and bath plans are final. Floor height ties into cabinet toe-kicks, appliance clearances, shower curbs and door swings.

What to plan around

Wood floors are easy to keep clean, but there are three things to plan for in an apartment:

  • Sound. Hard-soled shoes on wood carry to the unit below. Specify the underlayment or sound-rated assembly up front.
  • Scratches. Pick the finish, and put protector pads under furniture, with pets and daily traffic in mind.
  • Water. Keep wood out of long-term wet exposure, and fix leaks and humidity problems before installing.

Subfloor first

No floor outperforms what it sits on. Every manufacturer's installation instructions set limits for flatness, moisture and room conditions, and a floor installed outside them can void the warranty. Before a plank is ordered:

  1. Check flatness. Low spots in an older slab get leveled; high spots get ground down.
  2. Test moisture. A slab that reads high needs a plan before glue or wood goes on it. Fix the source first, whether that's a leaking line from the unit next door or a damp exterior wall in a brownstone.
  3. See what's there. Old adhesive, tile or a previous floor may have to come up, and that changes the budget.
  4. Condition the space. Bring the apartment to the manufacturer's temperature and humidity range and hold it there through installation.

This is where running all the trades from one office pays off. If plumbing is moving, the floor doesn't go down until the rough-in is finished and inspected. One schedule keeps the order right: rough plumbing and electrical, inspections, then finishes. That way nobody has to tear up a new floor to reach a pipe.

NYC rules: the law, the board and the lease

The law

Under Section 3(6) of the Multiple Dwelling Law, as published by the NYC Department of Buildings, a local law can't prohibit wood underflooring or finish flooring in a class A multiple dwelling erected after April 18, 1929, as long as it's installed in compliance with that chapter. In practice, the city isn't what stops a wood floor. Your building is.

The alteration agreement

In a co-op or condo, a new floor almost always needs approval under the alteration agreement. Buildings commonly set a sound requirement: a specific underlayment, or a tested rating for the assembly. Some engineered products also call for sound insulation between the slab and the boards. Read the agreement before you choose between glue-down engineered and fastened solid, because the install method and the sound assembly go together.

A typical board package for flooring asks for:

  • A description of the work and the floor assembly, with product data
  • The contractor's certificate of insurance naming the building
  • Work limited to the building's allowed days and hours
  • Freight elevator and loading reservations for materials and debris

We handle the paperwork that comes with that: DOB filings where the larger project needs them, alteration agreements, insurance certificates and work-hour limits. Renters

If you rent, get your landlord's written approval before changing a floor. Your lease controls what you can do.

Controlling noise for the neighbors below

Impact noise is what gets floors rejected and neighbors complaining, and you fix it in the floor assembly, not with a rug afterward.

  • Get the board's sound requirement in writing, whether it's a rating or a named underlayment.
  • Choose the underlayment and the flooring together as one assembly that meets it.
  • Keep the floor from touching walls and fixed elements. Wood needs that expansion gap anyway, and it also stops sound from bridging into the structure.
  • Do noisy work inside the building's allowed hours and give neighbors notice.

What apartment wood flooring costs

Species, thickness, finish, the building and the prep all move the number, and NYC labor, insurance and building logistics run well above national figures. Two published reference points:

  • Material, national: Consumer Reports lists the solid wood products it tested at $5 to $11 per square foot for the flooring alone.
  • NYC, as of 2020: Brick Underground, in a guide last published in March 2020, suggested budgeting around $8 per square foot for mid-range materials, $15 to $30 for high-end products, and $2 to $4 per square foot to remove and dispose of the old floor. Installation labor wasn't given because it varies so much.

Treat both as starting points, not quotes. Prices have moved since 2020, and labor, underlayment, slab leveling, trim, thresholds and freight-elevator time all come on top. The jobs that run over budget usually have a slab problem nobody recorded or a late change to the sound assembly.

We don't publish a flat price for flooring, because the slab, the building and the rest of the scope decide it. For a whole-apartment picture, see our gut renovation budget guide.

The installation sequence

A clean floor job in an occupied NYC building runs in this order:

  1. Walkthrough and measurements. Record the slab or subfloor condition, floor heights and thresholds.
  2. Board approval. Submit the alteration package with the floor assembly and insurance certificate.
  3. Demolition and removal. Take up the old floor and carry debris out on the building's terms.
  4. Subfloor prep. Level, dry and clean it to the manufacturer's limits.
  5. Rough trades and inspections. Finish anything that opens floors or walls before wood goes down.
  6. Acclimation. Hold the room in the manufacturer's temperature and humidity range.
  7. Underlayment and flooring. Glue-down or fastened, as the product and the board require.
  8. Trim and protection. Thresholds and baseboards, then cover the floor until the rest of the work is done.

Most failures come from doing things out of order: the floor goes in, then a plumber or electrician has to open it, or the painters follow with dust and tape. Flooring belongs near the end of a renovation for that reason.

Caring for wood floors in an apartment

The NWFA's homeowner's handbook keeps it practical:

  • Sweep, dust-mop or vacuum on the bare-floor setting to remove grit.
  • Put breathable rugs at doorways so debris isn't tracked in.
  • Clean spills right away with a dry or slightly damp cloth.
  • Don't use wet mops or steam mops.
  • Put protector pads under furniture legs that touch the floor.

Humidity matters as much as cleaning. The same handbook recommends keeping the home between 60 and 80°F and 30 to 50 percent humidity all year to limit seasonal gaps and swelling. In a New York apartment, that usually means a humidifier in winter when the radiators are running hot, and air conditioning or a dehumidifier in summer.

FAQ

Is engineered or solid wood better for a NYC apartment?

Engineered wood moves less with humidity and can often be glued to a flat, dry concrete slab. Solid wood needs a plywood subfloor over concrete, which raises the floor, but it can be refinished more times. The slab, the ceiling height and your building's rules usually decide.

How much does wood flooring cost per square foot?

Consumer Reports lists tested solid wood products at $5 to $11 per square foot nationally, for material only. A 2020 NYC guide from Brick Underground suggested about $8 for mid-range material and $15 to $30 for high-end, plus $2 to $4 for removal. Current NYC prices run higher, and labor and prep depend on the unit.

Do I need board approval for a new wood floor in a co-op or condo?

In most buildings, yes, under the alteration agreement. Boards commonly set a sound requirement for the floor assembly. Read the agreement before you choose the product or the install method.

What humidity is best for wood floors?

The NWFA recommends 60 to 80°F and 30 to 50 percent humidity year-round. Wood swells in humid air and shrinks in dry air, so steady conditions mean fewer gaps and less cupping.

Can I put in wood flooring in a rental?

Only with your landlord's written approval. Your lease controls what you can change.
