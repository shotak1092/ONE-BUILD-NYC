Bathroom Ventilation Installation in NYC: Sizing, Ducting, Code

How a bathroom exhaust fan is sized, ducted, wired and approved in an NYC renovation, from co-op shafts to brownstone roofs.

A bathroom fan works when four things are right: a fan sized to the room, a short, smooth duct that ends outdoors, a motor quiet enough that people leave it on, and wiring and approvals handled in the right order. In a New York apartment or brownstone the duct route is usually the hard part, because it has to get from the ceiling to the outdoors through joists, an existing shaft or a facade the building controls. That's why we plan the fan with the plumbing and electrical before demolition is finished, not after the tile is up.

What a bathroom ventilation installation includes

The fan is one part of a system. A complete installation has five:

  • The fan housing, fixed to framing in the ceiling or wall so it doesn't vibrate.
  • The duct, running from the fan to the outdoors or to an approved building exhaust.
  • The termination: a wall cap, roof vent or shaft connection with a damper that keeps outside air from coming back in.
  • Wiring and controls, including any light, timer or humidity sensor.
  • Approvals and inspection, which depend on your building and the scope of work.

Most guides stop at "cut a hole, mount the housing, connect the duct." In practice, the fan model rarely makes or breaks the result. The duct does.

What NYC code requires

New York City writes its own codes. The 2022 NYC Mechanical Code, Chapter 4 sets the exhaust rate for a bathroom or toilet room in a dwelling at 50 CFM where the fan runs intermittently, or 20 CFM where it runs continuously while the unit is occupied. The same table states that mechanical exhaust is required for these rooms and that recirculating the air is prohibited. A fan that blows bathroom air back into the apartment, or into a ceiling cavity, doesn't meet the intent.

Those are minimums. The practical sizing guidance below usually lands a little higher.

Choosing the right fan size

Start with CFM

CFM (cubic feet per minute) is how much air the fan moves. The Home Ventilating Institute gives these starting points:

Bathroom size Recommended fan rating
50 sq ft or smaller 50 CFM minimum
50 to 100 sq ft 1 CFM per sq ft of floor
Over 100 sq ft 50 CFM each for the toilet, shower and tub; 100 CFM for a jetted tub

A typical 5 by 8 foot prewar bathroom in Manhattan or Brooklyn is 40 sq ft, so 50 CFM is the floor.

Size up for real conditions

ENERGY STAR guidance, summarized by the Department of Energy's Building America Solution Center, recommends choosing a fan rated at least 70 CFM where the required intermittent rate is 50 CFM, and more than 50 CFM where the continuous rate is 20 CFM. The headroom covers duct resistance, which is never zero. A windowless bathroom with a long hot shower every morning is a good case for the higher end.

Check the spec sheet, not the box. A fan's rated airflow drops as duct resistance rises, so compare the airflow listed at realistic static pressure, not the headline number.

Pick a quiet fan

Loudness is measured in sones. The Home Ventilating Institute says a quiet bathroom fan should be rated at 1.0 sone or less, about the sound of a refrigerator. ENERGY STAR recommends 1 sone or less for a fan that runs continuously and 3 sones or less for one that runs intermittently. A loud fan gets switched off, and a fan that's off does nothing for moisture.

Controls worth specifying

A humidity sensor or a delay-off timer keeps the fan running after the shower ends without anyone remembering to leave it on. Both are easiest to wire while the ceiling is open.

Ducting: where most installations go wrong

Rules for the duct run

The Building America Solution Center's bathroom exhaust fan checklist lays out the essentials:

  • Take the most direct route outside with as few bends as possible, and no bends in the first three feet from the fan.
  • Keep the outdoor outlet at least 10 feet from any air inlet, and don't aim it at a walkway.
  • Seal all duct seams and connections with mastic or UL 181 tape, seal interior penetrations, and flash exterior ones.
  • Use at least the duct diameter the fan manufacturer specifies.

The U.S. Department of Energy's installation job aid adds that ducting should be 28-gauge or thicker, round metal connections should get at least three equally spaced screws, the exterior hole should be cut less than 1/2 inch larger than the duct, and duct through unconditioned space should be insulated to R-8 where the local authority requires it.

Why length and bends matter

Every foot of duct and every elbow adds resistance, and the fan's real airflow falls with it. Code tables limit duct length by fan rating and duct type, and each elbow uses up a large share of the allowance. In a prewar building where the duct has to work around beams, that allowance runs out fast. A larger duct or a stronger fan is the usual fix, and that call belongs in the plan, not in the middle of installation.

Where the duct can go in NYC buildings

  • Co-ops and condos in Manhattan and Queens high-rises: the fan usually ties into an existing exhaust shaft or a riser served by a roof fan. You can't change that connection on your own. The building's managing agent and engineer decide what's allowed, and it's written into the alteration agreement.
  • Brownstones and row houses in Brooklyn: a top-floor bathroom can often vent straight through the roof. Lower floors may need a run between joists to the rear wall, or a chase up through the floors above. In a landmarked district, a new opening on a visible facade can need Landmarks Preservation Commission review.
  • Houses on Staten Island, Long Island and in Westchester: a wall cap or roof vent is usually possible, but the duct still has to end outdoors. Never terminate it in an attic, a soffit or the space between floors.

Reusing an old duct

If you're replacing a fan on an existing duct, the Building America checklist says to confirm the duct is rigid metal with a smooth interior, has a working back-draft damper, is within the code's length limit and meets the fan's minimum diameter. If it fails any of those, replace it. A new fan on an old crushed or flexible duct will underperform, and in older buildings that's what we usually find.

Wiring and the steps that need a licensed electrician

Here's how the installation runs in a renovation, and where each step goes wrong:

  1. Locate the fan. Near the shower, between joists, with a clear path to the exhaust point. Bad placement forces extra elbows later.
  2. Frame and mount the housing. Fixed to framing so it doesn't rattle.
  3. Run the duct. Short, straight, supported, with sealed joints.
  4. Make the termination. Cap, roof vent or shaft connection with a damper, flashed and sealed. A badly flashed roof penetration is a leak waiting for the first storm.
  5. Wire the fan. New cable, switch or control, and the right circuit protection. In New York City this is licensed electrical work.
  6. Test and inspect. Check airflow and noise, and get the rough-in inspected before the ceiling closes.

On GFCI protection, EC&M's review of the National Electrical Code notes that the receptacle inside a bathroom exhaust fan doesn't need GFCI protection unless the fan's instructions call for it, but a fan installed directly over a tub or shower must be GFCI protected under its UL listing. Fans over the shower are common, so plan for protection and let the electrician confirm what your model requires.

Permits and building rules in NYC

New wiring for a fan is electrical work under the 2025 NYC Electrical Code, which took effect December 21, 2025. DOB sorts electrical filings into minor electrical work (permit only), electrical work (permit and DOB inspection), and work that also needs plan review. Which category applies depends on the scope, so the licensed electrician files based on what's actually changing.

The building adds its own layer. In a co-op or condo, expect an alteration agreement that covers the duct connection, a certificate of insurance naming the building and managing agent, limited work hours, and freight elevator bookings for materials. If the fan is part of a larger bathroom job that moves plumbing or walls, the DOB filing covers more than the fan. Our guide to bathroom remodeling costs, permits and timeline in New York walks through that.

What drives the cost

We don't publish a flat price for a fan installation because the job varies too much. These are the things that move a quote:

  • Duct route. A short run to an exterior wall is a different job from a long run to a roof or a new chase.
  • Termination. A roof penetration needs flashing. A wall cap or an existing shaft connection is simpler.
  • Fan type. A basic fan, a quiet high-efficiency model, or one with a light, humidity sensor and timer.
  • Wiring. A new switch run or circuit adds electrical work and a filing.
  • Access. An open ceiling in a gut renovation is far easier than fishing duct through a finished ceiling under a neighbor.
  • Building requirements. Alteration paperwork, insurance, engineer review and work-hour limits.

In a renovation, the fan is a small line inside the bathroom budget. A bathroom gut remodel with basic finishes in NYC starts at $15,000 to $25,000, and the ceiling is already open, which is the cheapest time to get the fan and duct right.

Why ventilation belongs in the renovation plan

The duct route, the wiring path and the finished ceiling all depend on each other. If the fan is an afterthought, the tile is up, the ceiling is closed and the only route left is the bad one. That's how a 50 CFM fan ends up on a long, bent run that can't move the air it was bought to move.

Order matters too. Framing, rough plumbing and electrical come first, then inspections, then close-up and finishes. Our walkthrough of the right order of trades in a bathroom renovation shows where the fan fits.

FAQ

What size bathroom exhaust fan do I need?

For a bathroom of 50 sq ft or less, the Home Ventilating Institute recommends at least 50 CFM. For 50 to 100 sq ft, use about 1 CFM per square foot. Go higher if the duct is long or has several elbows, or if the bathroom has a steam-heavy shower.

What does NYC code require for bathroom exhaust?

The NYC Mechanical Code sets 50 CFM for intermittent operation or 20 CFM for continuous operation in a dwelling bathroom, requires mechanical exhaust, and prohibits recirculating the air.

Can a bathroom fan vent into the ceiling or attic?

No. Moist air dumped into a ceiling cavity or attic leads to mold and rotted framing. The duct has to end outdoors or at an approved building exhaust.

Can I connect a new fan to my building's exhaust shaft?

Only with the building's approval. In co-ops and condos the shaft is a shared system, and the managing agent or building engineer decides what can connect to it. That approval goes into your alteration agreement.

How quiet should a bathroom fan be?

Look for 1.0 sone or less. The Home Ventilating Institute compares that to a refrigerator.
