Licensed Renovation Contractor: How to Hire One in NYC

What a licensed renovation contractor must hold in NYC, how to check it with DCWP and DOB, and what the contract must say before you sign.

In New York City, "licensed" isn't one card. A renovation contractor working on a home needs a Home Improvement Contractor license from the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection (DCWP), plumbing has to be done by a Licensed Master Plumber, electrical work runs under a licensed master electrician, and many jobs need Department of Buildings (DOB) permits on top of that. This guide covers each layer, how to check it, what the contract has to include under city rules, and the red flags that cost owners the most. The three layers of "licensed" in NYC

A renovation can touch all three of these. Ask how each one is covered on your job.

The company license. DCWP requires a Home Improvement Contractor license for anyone doing construction, repair or remodeling on residential property in the city. To get one, a company has to show workers' compensation coverage, either enroll in the city's Home Improvement Business Trust Fund or post a $20,000 surety bond, have a principal pass the Home Improvement Exam, and have its owners and officers fingerprinted. That's on DCWP's license checklist. The license covers cabinets and floor resurfacing too: DOB notes that those jobs don't need a permit but the contractor still needs a DCWP license.

The licensed trades. Plumbing and electrical are separately licensed by DOB. According to DOB's permit guidance, even plumbing work that doesn't need a permit must be performed by a Licensed Master Plumber. Electrical work is done under a DOB-licensed master electrician. A contractor with a company license who hands plumbing to an unlicensed helper has created a problem that shows up at inspection, at resale or after a leak.

The permits. DOB says most construction in the city needs its approval and permits. Moving walls, rerouting plumbing, adding circuits and upgrading a panel generally fall into that category. Painting, plastering and fixture swaps generally don't. Your contractor should tell you at the walkthrough which permits apply and who files them.

How to verify a contractor before you sign

This takes under an hour.

  • Get the license number in writing. City rules require it on the contract, along with the business name exactly as it appears on the DCWP license.
  • Check it. DCWP says to call 311 or visit nyc.gov/dcwp to check a license's status. DOB's Know Your Construction Professional page links to its license search for master plumbers and master electricians.
  • Ask who holds the trade licenses on your job. You want the names of the licensed plumber and electrician, not "our guys."
  • Ask for a certificate of insurance. Co-op and condo boards will require one naming the building and managing agent, so a contractor who has it ready has worked in buildings before.
  • Confirm one point of contact. Someone has to answer when the plumber and the tile setter disagree about a Tuesday.

One practical reason the license matters: DCWP says the Trust Fund may repay you if a licensed contractor doesn't finish the job or owes you money. That protection doesn't exist if you hire an unlicensed contractor.

What a NYC home improvement contract has to include

DCWP spells out what a compliant contract contains. Use its contractual compliance checklist when you compare proposals. Among the items:

  • The contractor's licensed business name, address, phone number and license number.
  • Approximate start and completion dates, and what could change the completion date.
  • A specific description of the work and materials, including brands and model numbers, with prices.
  • A progress-payment schedule where each payment bears a reasonable relationship to the work done.
  • A clause requiring the contractor to provide a workers' compensation certificate before work begins and to obtain all required permits.
  • A notice about mechanic's lien rights.
  • A boldface statement that you can cancel before midnight of the third business day, plus a separate Notice of Cancellation form.
  • A permit notice, given on or before signing, that lists the permits needed, who gets them, estimated costs and how to check permit status in city databases.

On top of the legal minimum, a proposal worth signing also lists allowances for tile, fixtures and appliances, a schedule with milestones, and a written change-order process.

Red flags that cost owners the most

  • No license number, or one that doesn't match the company on the contract.
  • "Permits by owner" on work that clearly needs them.
  • No insurance certificate, or a promise to "send it later."
  • A lump sum with no scope breakdown, so every change becomes a dispute.
  • A large payment up front. DCWP's hiring tips advise paying no more than 25% of the contract to start, and never paying cash or without a contract.
  • Plumbing and electrical "included" with no named licensed trade behind them.
  • No answer on how work hours, elevator reservations and hallway protection will be handled in your building.

None of these is about price. They're signs the project's structure is thin, and structure is what protects you when something turns up behind a wall. In a prewar Manhattan co-op or a Brooklyn brownstone, something usually does.

Why coordination matters as much as the license

A license tells you a company may legally do the work. It doesn't tell you the work will happen in the right order, and that's where renovations go wrong.

Take a bathroom. The sequence is demolition, plumbing and electrical rough-in, inspection, then closing walls, waterproofing, tile, fixtures and finishes. If the plumber is booked separately and shows up a week late, the tile setter waits, the inspection slips, and a building's weekday-only work window turns one lost week into three. Add new circuits, a gas line, ventilation and cabinetry in a kitchen, and owners with separate vendors end up acting as the project manager.

What the building adds

Houses and small buildings. In a Queens two-family or a Staten Island colonial, you mainly deal with the city: permits where the scope needs them, rough-in inspections and sign-off. Our guide to home renovation inspections in NYC covers what gets checked and when.

Co-ops and condos. In Manhattan and Brooklyn apartment buildings you deal with the city and the board. Before demolition, most buildings require:

  • An alteration agreement setting the rules for your renovation.
  • A certificate of insurance naming the building and managing agent.
  • Approved work hours, usually a limited weekday window.
  • Freight elevator reservations, floor protection and debris rules.

We handle that paperwork as part of the job, so the managing agent has one contact and one insurance package. Our condo renovation contractor guide goes deeper on the board side.

Budget and contingency

Older New York buildings hide things: outdated wiring, galvanized supply lines, out-of-level floors. A realistic budget carries a contingency for them, and the contract should say how changes are priced and approved in writing before the extra work starts.

FAQ

Is a licensed home improvement contractor the same as a licensed plumber or electrician?

No. In NYC the company doing residential renovation holds a DCWP Home Improvement Contractor license, while plumbing and electrical work require DOB-licensed master plumbers and electricians. Ask for the company license number and who holds the trade licenses on your job.

How do I check a contractor's license in NYC?

For the DCWP Home Improvement Contractor license, call 311 or visit nyc.gov/dcwp. For master plumbers and electricians, use the license search linked from DOB's Know Your Construction Professional page.

Do I need permits for a renovation in NYC?

It depends on the scope. DOB says most construction needs its approval. Work that changes walls, plumbing, gas or electrical systems generally does; painting, plastering and fixture replacement generally don't. Your contractor should tell you which apply and who files them.

How much should I pay up front?

DCWP advises paying no more than 25% of the total contract to get the work started, never in cash and never without a signed contract.

What do co-op and condo boards require from a contractor?

Typically an alteration agreement, a certificate of insurance naming the building, approved work hours and rules for elevators and common areas. Requirements vary by building, so get the managing agent's package early.
