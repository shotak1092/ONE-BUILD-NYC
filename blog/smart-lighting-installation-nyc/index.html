Smart Lighting Installation in NYC: Plan, Wire, Set Up

Smart lighting installation in an NYC apartment or brownstone: zones, neutral wires, dimmer compatibility, DOB permits, setup steps and cost drivers.

Smart lighting works when it is planned before the walls close. Zones, switch locations, neutral wires, dimmers and network coverage get decided together, so the system works on day one and nobody opens finished plaster to fix it. In a New York apartment or townhouse, the DOB permit, the building's paperwork and its work hours belong in the same schedule. Here is what the job involves, what to check in your wiring, how it is sequenced and what moves the price.

Two different jobs called "smart lighting"

People use the phrase for two very different things.

The first is swapping in connected bulbs and running them from an app. It suits a floor lamp, or a rental you plan to leave.

The second is a whole-home system: wired smart switches, dimmers or keypads replace the standard ones, circuits are grouped into zones, and scenes like "dinner," "movie" or "away" are programmed. That is the job this guide covers, and it is where planning matters.

A dedicated hub isn't always required, but on a larger system it usually pays for itself in range, reliability and automations that keep running when the internet goes down. In an apartment with plaster walls, steel studs or a long hallway of recessed lights, design that reliability in from the start.

If you are planning a full keypad-and-panel system, our guide to lighting control installation in NYC goes deeper on design, commissioning and handoff.

Plan the zones before you buy anything

Start with how you live, not with a product list. Walk each room and think about 7 a.m., dinner and 11 p.m.

Map each group of lights to its job: ambient, task or accent. Keep groups with different jobs on separate controls, put the controls you use most at the room's entrance, and add a second control location wherever a route has two ways in. Don't overdo it. Too many zones make daily use confusing.

A typical layout:

  • Kitchen: recessed ambient lights, pendants over the island, under-cabinet task lights. Three zones, each dimmable on its own. See our notes on kitchen lighting installation in NYC.
  • Living room: ceiling lights, a cove or wall-wash accent, and lamps. One keypad at the entrance with four or five scenes is easier to live with than a bank of ten switches.
  • Bedroom: a main light, bedside lights you can control from bed, and a low path light to the bathroom.
  • Hallways and stairs: control at both ends.
  • Bathrooms: vanity and ambient on separate controls, plus a low night setting.

Then pick one platform and stay on it. Confirm that every device works with that platform before you buy it, rather than assuming it will. A whole-home system is the opposite of a drawer full of impulse buys.

Check wiring, neutrals and dimmer compatibility

This is where most installs succeed or stumble, and you can't see it until a cover plate comes off.

Neutral wires

Many smart switches and dimmers need a neutral wire to power their electronics. A lot of switch boxes in prewar Manhattan buildings and Brooklyn brownstones were wired the old way, with a hot and a switched leg and no neutral. Requirements vary by product, so check the installation instructions for the exact device you plan to use.

In a gut renovation the answer is simple: run a neutral to every control location while the walls are open. In an occupied apartment, the electrician opens and surveys each box first, then picks devices that fit what is there.

Bulbs, drivers and dimmers

Identify the bulb or fixture type, confirm the dimmer is made for it, and keep the connected load inside the dimmer's rated range. LED lamps have to be marked dimmable, and it is worth checking both manufacturers' compatibility lists.

Treat the fixture, its LED driver (the small power supply inside an LED fixture), the dimmer and the control as one system. "Dimmable" on the box doesn't guarantee smooth dimming. Mismatches show up as flicker, buzzing, a narrow dimming range, a delay when you press the button, or a faint glow when the light is off.

Existing fixtures and wiring

Before any device is ordered, record:

  • Which box feeds which lights, and how many fixtures are on each circuit.
  • The bulb or driver type for each fixture, including old transformers behind low-voltage lights.
  • Three-way and four-way circuits, where one light is controlled from more than one spot.
  • Whether there is a neutral and a ground at each box.
  • Wi-Fi or hub signal strength at each control location.

Record the existing connections and follow the device's wiring diagram. Don't guess from wire colors.

When you need a licensed electrician

Plugging in a lamp module is simple. Replacing a wall switch is electrical work, and in New York City it is regulated. The Department of Buildings says no installation or modification of an electrical system can be performed without a permit and a Licensed Master Electrician. Even replacing defective controls of 30 amps and under counts as minor work that needs a licensed master electrician and a permit, though not a DOB inspection. New circuits and new control locations are general electrical work, which needs a permit and a DOB inspection.

There is also a safety reason. Turning off a wall switch doesn't make the wiring behind it safe; the breaker does. In older NYC buildings you find decades of patched wiring, cloth-insulated conductors and shared neutrals. If the wires can't be identified, or the power can't be confirmed off, stop.

How the installation runs in a renovation

The sequence matters more than any single product. In a renovation, it usually goes like this:

  1. Walkthrough and lighting plan. Rooms, zones, control locations, fixture types and the platform get decided with the designer or homeowner.
  2. Permits and building approval. The electrical permit is filed with the DOB and, in a co-op or condo, the board signs off.
  3. Rough-in. With the walls open, electricians run circuits, neutrals and control cabling, plus any low-voltage wire for keypads, sensors and shades. Our guides on what to pre-wire before the drywall goes up and smart home wiring in NYC list what to run.
  4. Inspection. Plumbing, electrical and framing are scheduled in order so inspections happen before the walls close.
  5. Finishes and devices. Drywall, paint and tile go in, then fixtures, dimmers, keypads and sensors.
  6. Setup. Devices are joined to the hub or network, named by room, assigned to zones and tuned: low and high trim levels, fade times.
  7. Scenes and schedules. Scenes are programmed to the household's routine, with schedules for entry and exterior lights.
  8. Testing and handoff. Every circuit, every multi-location switch and every scene is tested. You are shown the keypads, the app and the manual override.

When one project lead schedules every trade, the lighting plan doesn't collide with the cabinets, the tile layout or the motorized shades.

NYC permits, buildings and work hours

The code. Local Law 128 of 2024 enacted the 2025 NYC Electrical Code, which took effect on December 21, 2025 and applies to projects filed on or after that date.

Permit renewals. Starting June 29, 2026, the DOB added new filing questions and requirements for electrical work under that code. According to a DOB construction advisory, electrical permits are now valid for up to one year and must be renewed annually until the job is signed off, with a $130 fee each time. That matters on a long renovation. Ask your contractor who tracks the renewal date.

Rules and fees change, so check the DOB's Electrical Code page for the current process.

The building. Co-op and condo boards usually want an alteration agreement before work starts, plus certificates of insurance, and they set work hours and freight elevator times. In many Manhattan and Queens buildings, that paperwork takes longer than the install. Our guide to the NYC co-op and condo alteration process walks through it. We handle the DOB filings, alteration requirements and insurance certificates, so your building deals with one contact.

What drives the cost

We don't publish a flat price for smart lighting, because the scope varies too much for one number to mean anything. These are what move a quote:

  • Control points and zones. Ten dimmers and two keypads is a different job from forty circuits with scene control in every room.
  • Wiring condition. Missing neutrals, shallow old boxes and shared circuits add labor. Open walls make this far cheaper than fishing wire through finished plaster.
  • Fixture and driver types. Cove lighting, specialty fixtures and low-voltage transformers need matched dimming.
  • Platform and hub. A larger system may need a dedicated hub and network upgrades.
  • Integration. Tying lighting to shades, audio or a home theater adds programming time.
  • Building rules. Restricted work hours, elevator bookings and insurance requirements change the schedule and the labor.
  • Programming and handoff. Scene design, tuning and training take real time.

The cheapest time to add smart lighting is during a renovation, when wire runs cost a fraction of a retrofit. A walkthrough gets you a real number.

Making the system reliable

Most problems trace back to planning, not the app.

  • Flicker or buzzing: usually a bulb, driver and dimmer mismatch, or a load below the dimmer's minimum.
  • Glow when off: often a driver and dimmer pairing problem.
  • Slow or dropped response: weak signal near the device. Put the hub centrally and add access points where needed.
  • Nothing works when the internet drops: choose a system with local control, so switches, keypads and schedules keep running.
  • Confusing to use: too many zones. Consolidate and keep keypad labels simple.
  • Devices won't pair: platform mismatch. Confirm compatibility before buying.

If the fault points to the wiring, like a hot switch box, a breaker that keeps tripping or a burning smell, turn off the breaker and call a licensed electrician.

FAQ

Can I put a smart bulb in any lamp?

A smart bulb fits any lamp with the right base and wattage rating, but it needs constant power. If the light is on a wall switch or dimmer, turning that switch off cuts the bulb's connection. For lights controlled from the wall, wired smart switches and dimmers are the better fit.

Do I need a hub for whole-home smart lighting?

Not always. On a larger system, a hub usually improves range and reliability and keeps automations running without internet. Decide during planning, because it affects device choice and network design.

Does smart lighting need a neutral wire?

Many smart switches and dimmers do, but it depends on the product. Check the instructions for the exact device and confirm what is in each box before choosing. In a renovation, we run neutrals while the walls are open.

Do I need a permit to install smart switches in NYC?

Yes. The DOB says electrical installation or modification requires a permit and a Licensed Master Electrician, and even replacing defective controls is minor work that needs both. Co-ops and condos also require building approval.

When should I stop and call an electrician?

If you can't identify the wires, can't confirm the power is off, or find old, damaged or unexpected wiring. Turning off a wall switch doesn't guarantee the wires are dead; use the breaker.
