Gut Renovation Contractor in NYC: How to Hire the Right One

How to hire a gut renovation contractor in NYC: licenses, DOB permits, the new permit disclosure rule, contract terms and why one team for all trades matters.

The right gut renovation contractor in New York City is a licensed firm that runs every trade under one contract and one schedule, handles the Department of Buildings (DOB) paperwork and your building's alteration rules, and puts scope, payments and warranty in writing. This guide covers what the job includes, where gut renovations go wrong, what to verify before you sign, the NYC rules that changed this year, and what the contract should say.

What a gut renovation contractor handles

A gut renovation takes a space down to studs and subfloor: finishes, partitions, old wiring and usually supply and drain lines. Then the space is rebuilt, often to a new layout. It's a different job from a kitchen refresh or a bathroom update, and it's the one where coordination matters most.

A contractor running a full gut should cover:

  • Demolition and protection. Taking the space down while protecting hallways, elevators and neighbors.
  • Framing and layout changes. New partitions, closets, openings and ceiling heights.
  • Plumbing. New supply, drain and fixture rough-ins.
  • Electrical. New circuits, a panel upgrade where the load calls for it, lighting and outlets.
  • Low-voltage. Network, audio, shade pockets and lighting control wiring, which cost far less to run while the walls are open.
  • Close-up and finishes. Insulation, drywall, tile, flooring, cabinetry and paint.
  • Permits, inspections and building paperwork.

That last item is where most owners get surprised. In New York City the construction is half the project. The other half is filings, board approvals, insurance certificates and a building's rules on when work is allowed. Our gut renovation page shows how we scope it.

The stages, and where they go wrong

The order of work is set by physics and by inspectors. Trades can't swap places without cost.

  1. Walkthrough, scope and design. Layout, fixtures, appliances, lighting and technology get decided before demolition. Changes here are cheap. Changes after the walls close are not.
  2. Approvals. DOB filings and, in a co-op or condo, the board's alteration agreement. These can take longer than the construction, so they start first.
  3. Asbestos assessment and demolition. The DOB requires an asbestos assessment before a permit for buildings constructed before April 1, 1987. That covers most prewar apartments, brownstones and older co-ops in Manhattan and Brooklyn.
  4. Rough-in. Framing, then plumbing, electrical and low-voltage while everything is exposed.
  5. Inspections. Work is inspected before it's covered. Close a wall early and it gets opened again.
  6. Close-up and finishes. Insulation, drywall, tile, floors, cabinets, paint and fixtures.
  7. Final inspection, punch list and sign-off.

The usual failure is sequencing. The plumber is booked a week after the electrician needs the same wall. The inspector shows up and the next trade isn't ready. A shade pocket or speaker location gets forgotten and the drywall is already up. When separate companies hold separate contracts, nobody owns those gaps. When one office schedules every trade, the gaps are the project lead's problem, not yours. Our guide to renovation inspections in NYC goes deeper on the inspection side.

Licenses and insurance to verify before you sign

Start with the license. The city's Department of Consumer and Worker Protection says a person or business must have a Home Improvement Contractor license to do construction, repair, remodeling or other home improvement work on residential property in New York City. Ask for the number and confirm it with the agency. Ours is NYC HIC License #621290.

Trade licenses matter too. The DOB's permit guidance says plumbing and gas work, even ordinary replacement and relocation, must be performed by a Licensed Master Plumber. A general contractor who hands plumbing to an unknown sub puts your approvals at risk along with your pipes.

FEMA's contractor checklist is a good vetting routine:

  • Verify the license with the issuing agency.
  • Ask how many building permits the contractor has obtained in your jurisdiction in the last two years.
  • Confirm general liability insurance and check that the policy is valid.
  • Contact three to five past clients with similar work.
  • Expect a written warranty of at least one year on work and materials, with two years preferred.

The National Association of Realtors' consumer guide adds four good questions: are the workers employees or subcontractors, will the contractor secure all permits and approvals, what allowances are in the bid, and how will they communicate with you during the job.

In a co-op or condo, add one more: a certificate of insurance that meets the building's alteration rules. With us, the building deals with one contact and one insurance package, not a certificate from each trade.

NYC permit rules that changed

Most construction in New York City needs DOB approval and permits. Two changes are worth knowing.

The permit disclosure (in effect since June 19, 2026). Under Local Law 181 of 2025, a home improvement contractor must give you a written notice, no later than the day the contract is signed, listing which permits are required, who is responsible for getting them, the steps involved including estimated fees, and how you can check permit status in city databases. You sign and date it. The city publishes the disclosure form, and DCWP's implementing rule took effect June 19, 2026. If a proposal arrives without it, ask for it.

The Existing Building Code (applies to filings from July 17, 2027). The DOB says the new code, enacted as Local Law 33 of 2026, will govern alteration applications filed on and after July 17, 2027. It classifies alterations by work area rather than cost, and it creates a category of Limited Home Improvement Alterations, some of which a homeowner can file. Plumbing and electrical work tied to them still needs a permit and a licensed plumber or electrician. If your project files before mid-2027, current rules apply.

If you own a co-op or condo unit, the board's process runs alongside the city's. Our guide to the co-op and condo alteration process walks through it, and hiring a condo renovation contractor covers what boards want to see.

What the contract should say

A gut renovation is too big for a handshake or a one-page estimate. In joint guidance, the National Association of Home Builders and NARI call for a detailed written contract with:

  • A clear scope of work.
  • A payment schedule.
  • A process for changes.
  • Start and finish dates.
  • Warranties.

They advise paying no more than one-third upfront, with later payments tied to milestones. NARI's contractor checklist puts the deposit at no more than 25 to 30 percent (it varies by state), says never to pay in cash, and recommends lien waivers from subcontractors and suppliers plus a final walkthrough with a punch list. Its advice to get at least three written estimates, each with scope, material specifications, labor costs, timeline and payment schedule, is how you compare bids fairly.

Here's what a milestone schedule can look like. It illustrates the idea; it isn't a quote:

Milestone What has to be finished
Signing Contract, scope and permit disclosure signed
Demolition complete Space at studs, debris removed
Rough-in passed Plumbing, electrical and low-voltage inspected
Close-up complete Walls, ceilings and subfloor ready for finishes
Finishes installed Tile, cabinets, fixtures, paint
Final sign-off Punch list done, final inspections closed

When you compare bids, compare scope line by line. A lower number often leaves out permits, fixture allowances, low-voltage wiring or protection of common areas.

Why one team for every trade matters

Most gut renovation problems come from handoffs, not workmanship. Hire a general contractor, a plumber, an electrician and a smart home installer separately and you become the project manager. Your designer chases four schedules. Your managing agent collects four insurance certificates and enforces work hours on four crews.

A design-build firm holds the trades together. With construction, plumbing, electrical and low-voltage under one contract, rough-in, inspections and finishes happen in the right order on one schedule. It also changes the technology conversation. Lighting control, motorized shades, audio and networking are planned before the walls close, so wire runs, shade pockets and equipment locations are in the drawings instead of cut into finished walls.

  • Homeowners get one contract, one lead and one number to call.
  • Property managers and boards get one insurance package and one party accountable for work hours and alteration rules.
  • Designers and architects get one team that builds what the drawings show, low-voltage included.
  • Investors get a sequenced schedule, which shortens the time a unit sits empty.

What drives the cost

Price depends on building type, floor area, finish level and whether layout or structure changes. The biggest drivers are the number of kitchens and bathrooms, how much plumbing moves, electrical load and panel work, asbestos testing and abatement in older buildings, building work hours and hauling logistics, and the level of technology. FAQ

Do I need a licensed contractor for a gut renovation in NYC?

Yes. Residential remodeling in New York City requires a Home Improvement Contractor license from DCWP, and the DOB requires a Licensed Master Plumber for plumbing and gas work. Check each license with the agency that issued it before you sign.

Who pulls the permits, me or the contractor?

The contract should say. Since June 19, 2026, your home improvement contractor must give you a signed written notice listing the permits required, who obtains each one, estimated fees and how to check status. At One Build NYC, DOB permits are part of the project.

How should I pay for a gut renovation?

In milestones tied to finished work. NAHB and NARI say no more than about one-third upfront, NARI's checklist suggests 25 to 30 percent at most, and never in cash. Collect lien waivers and finish with a punch-list walkthrough.

Is a gut renovation different in a co-op or condo?

Yes. On top of DOB approvals, you need board approval through an alteration agreement, insurance certificates naming the building, and compliance with its work hours and freight elevator rules. A contractor who knows the process saves weeks of back-and-forth.

Does an older building change the process?

Often. Buildings constructed before April 1, 1987 need an asbestos assessment before the DOB issues a permit, and prewar walls and floors tend to hide conditions that only show up at demolition. Budget time and contingency for both.

Why plan smart home and lighting during the gut?

The walls are open, so network, lighting control and shade wiring cost far less to run now than later. It also puts the drawings, the electrician and the low-voltage team on the same plan.
